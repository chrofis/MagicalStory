# YOUR TASK

Judge the plan as a reader who sees only these pages. The brief and character details tell you what the book is FOR; they are never evidence that a page works. A motivation that lives only in the character details and never reaches a beat is a fault to fix.

Answer each check, naming pages. Then rewrite only the pages that a check found wrong. A fault you name must change a page: end your analysis with a line per named fault — the fault, then the page(s) your rewrite fixes it on, or the reason it stands. A named fault with neither is a failed review. Every AUDIT FINDING above enters that ledger the same way: fixed on a page, or a stated reason it stands.

1. Causality — for each page, does its beat follow from the page before? Name the most arbitrary transition, and anything impossible in space or time as written — a thing followed, found or known before it exists, someone in two places, an act that cannot be done while holding what they hold. Name any character who is present and then simply gone with no beat saying where, and anything a beat established about a thing — stuck, heavy, hidden, out of reach — that a later beat quietly reverses.
2. Arc — read the committed arc against the pages. Name the page where a character says aloud what must happen and why; a look or a glance is not a statement, and if no page says it, rewrite the earliest fitting one so it does. Name every page it claims carries a turn that the beat there does not deliver, and every main character whose stated turning point or change never happens on the page. Name the page where the want first meets something that will not simply move, the page where a try fails or costs them something and the goal sits further away, and the page where the change is earned; when the arc itself names none of these, say so. Rewrite the fewest beats to deliver the arc, or to supply what neither the arc nor the pages contain.
3. Stakes — name the page where the least is at risk and rewrite it so something is at risk. Name every obstacle removed in the beat that introduces it, and every blocker who yields without wanting something of their own; rewrite them so passing costs something the beat names. Frightening is the right level for the youngest named child: name any peril that could kill or injure one — drowning, a fall from a height — and rewrite it to a cost that is not to the body, such as a refusal, a loss, a delay or a promise broken.
4. Illustratable — name any scene that cannot be one drawn moment: a montage, a passage of time, an internal state with nothing to see, or an empty stage where a beat needs a character present. Also name any scene that is one moment but a HARD one — two characters doing different things at one object, a realization or decision as the visual, an acrobatic/climbing pose with occluded limbs, fine mechanism manipulation, or several characters running parallel unrelated actions instead of reacting to one focal event, or a group with one shared goal split across separate spots with separate mini-actions — they do the same thing in the same place. Rewrite those into their nearest simple tableau (the visible result of the action, or one character holding/showing the object). Name any scene that shows the aftermath of its beat's action rather than the action itself, and any scene that composes the frame — what fills it, how large something looks, where it sits. A shot type is allowed.
5. Repetition — name any two pages whose scenes would produce near-identical pictures (same place, same grouping, same action), and any one gathering split across two pages. A place used for several pages is fine when each page shows a different picture of it; a run of pages that all show figures standing or talking on the way somewhere is not. Apply this to the pages you are rewriting as well as the ones you leave: a rewrite that restages a place already used, or spreads a single huddle over two pages, is the most common source.
6. Cast & variety — name any scene with more than {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE} named characters, or any page where someone forces or blocks something without being in the scene. Do most pages keep to three or fewer named characters, and does the cast vary page to page — the main character on most pages, the others in changing combinations? When the whole cast appears, is it a wide or distant view rather than a close full-cast line-up? Does the book include some framing variety — a characterless establishing view, a distant-small-figures page, a single-character close-up? A close-up beat's moment must work waist-up (holding, reading, reacting) — flag one built on kneeling or floor action. Flag any page separating a figure into the background without the beat requiring the separation — bring them to the group. Name any beat using more named characters than it needs, any invented figure the story could do without — one an existing character could replace or absorb — and any split or reunion with no stated reason. Name what's missing.
6b. Division — does each part of the arc get the pages the STORY SHAPE allows it, and does the book hold that proportion? Name any challenge squeezed into one page, any moment given more pages than it earns, and any stretch of pages where the story does not move. Name the page each character first appears on: they do not all arrive at once.
6c. Drawability of the division — read the PAGE PLAN against the beats: name any page whose beat no longer matches its planned shot or cast count, any page carrying a group action that would be a stronger book split across pages (one face close, then a pair, then the rest), any run of pages with the same shot and the same number of characters, and say so if fewer than a third of the pages hold one character or none.
7. Pacing — name the page carrying the most beats and the one carrying the least, and every beat that packs a sequence of actions rather than one change; rewrite those down to the single change and move the rest to the page that follows. Name any deadline or cost the arc states that no page ever shows.
7b. Local landmarks — when an AVAILABLE LANDMARKS list is present, name the pages that could plausibly be staged at one of those real places and are not. Rewrite the fewest beats to move that action there; say so explicitly when the story genuinely cannot reach any of them.
7c. Delivery — name any item on the arc's Closed list that no page delivers, and any challenge or moment the arc commits to that the pages dropped. Name the subject the book is about if any stretch of pages loses it to a hint — a shadow, a sound, eyes in the dark — where the STORY SHAPE requires it on the page.
8. Loose threads & unearned turns — name any question a beat explicitly raises about the plot (who took it, why, where it came from) that no later beat answers. Name any object, clue, ally, or rescue that resolves the conflict but appears with no stated origin or in-world licence — a rule, a legend, or an earlier setup. Whoever receives or resolves the ending is established before the final third; a receiver who first appears at the handover is rewritten, not accepted. A reason the plan asserts is not a reason it grounded: flag every sign, inscription or rule announced once to license a turn, every figure whose role and holdings are declared rather than established, and rewrite so the reason follows from who someone is, what a place is for, or what someone needs. Name any character singled out ("the only one who can", "was waited for", "chosen") with no reason given. Name any contradiction the plan inherited from the idea instead of resolving it — one object both already held and still sought, an arrival with no cause. Name any obstacle shaped around a thing a character carries — a barrier whose only solution is something one of them already holds — and any obstacle that does not arise from the established setting. Name any prop, pet or skill the plan introduces that never does work later, and either give it work or cut the introduction. A trait in the character details is not an introduction: a prop, pet or skill must appear in an earlier scene before it solves anything. Rewrite the fewest beats needed to close each — add the answering line, the origin, or the reason, and resolve rather than repeat.
9. Emotion — name any beat where a character's mistake, loss, or triumph passes without a felt reaction (a look, a word, a gesture in the scene). Name the page where the lesson is stated or shown without a character visibly feeling it. Rewrite those beats so the feeling is a drawable moment, not a summary.
10. Theme — does the story's stated life-skill or topic drive the climax, and does the character who most needs the lesson visibly act on it by the end? Name the page where the lesson is earned; if it is only implied, rewrite the fewest beats to make one character acknowledge or apply it.
