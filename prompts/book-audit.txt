You are reading a finished picture book the way its audience receives it: an adult reads a page aloud while the child looks at that page's picture. Below you get, in reading order, each page's text, then the brief its picture was drawn from — the moment it shows and the characters it was drawn with — then the picture.

This batch covers page(s) {PAGE_LIST}.

The main character is: {MAIN_CHARACTERS}. Every question that says "the main character" is judged against exactly that character, never against whoever the title names first.

The words and the picture tell the story together. Some pages carry the story mostly in the picture and use few words; others carry it mostly in the words. That balance is never a fault. Never report a page for having little text, sparse action, or a quiet moment.

The brief decides who is in the picture, and where it lists what each drawn figure wears, judge a figure's garments against its own entry, never against a garment you saw on another figure. A picture shows one moment, and the words may carry others: a character the words name but the brief did not draw is absent by design, and a text that has them act, hold, listen or stand beside the drawn figures while the brief leaves them out is the picture's choice of whom to draw, never a fault in either. This never covers the main character, nor a figure whose act the page's words make the page's event: when the picture leaves out either one at the moment it shows, question 1 judges it. For every other character, report a presence fault only as FAULT[TEXT][MINOR] and only where one quoted sentence says the character is IN the instant the picture shows, as the thing to look at on this page ("there he stands, in the doorway"), and the picture shows that place empty. Quote that sentence in the fault. A character named while the words speak of elsewhere, before or after, or acting beside the drawn figures, is no fault.

Where a brief carries a HEIGHT ORDER line, it is the order and the size gaps the painter was asked for. Report a height fault (type `height_order`, weight MAJOR) when two named figures who both stand upright on the same ground, at about the same distance from the viewer, are drawn in the wrong order against HEIGHT ORDER, or when the gap between them is far bigger than HEIGHT ORDER gives — for example one a whole head taller where the line puts the shorter one's head top at the taller one's forehead or eyebrows. Figures whose feet are at clearly different heights in the picture stand at different distances and are not compared. Never compare a figure that sits, kneels, crouches, leans, or stands clearly further back or on a different surface; if no pair can be compared, there is no fault.

First read the whole book once, in order, words and pictures together, and answer question 1 for the book as a whole. It is the first job of this review: a book whose pages each look right but do not add up to one story that makes sense has failed.
1. CONSISTENCY: where does the book stop making sense? Look for each of these:
- {LOGIC_CONTRADICTION}
- {LOGIC_PLACE}
- {LOGIC_KNOWLEDGE}
- {LOGIC_OBSTACLE} That act is told on its page and drawn there, performed by the main character.
- A picture contradicts the story so far: a figure the story has present at this page's moment is missing from the picture (the main character on a page whose words have them act, decide or feel in that moment, or the figure whose act is the page's event), a figure the story has somewhere else is drawn here, or the picture shows a state the story has not reached yet or has already left behind.
For each one, decide what is the smallest change that makes the book make sense, and report that one only. Write FAULT[TEXT] when different words on one page fix it; its sentence is the order to the editor and names the words to change ("Say that she climbs in through the window before she stands in the room"). The words may change; what happens in the story does not. {NO_NEW_ELEMENT} Write FAULT[IMG] with its route when a different picture fixes it ("Draw the main character holding the key out to the lock"); a figure who belongs at the moment and is missing is `missing_character`, routed NEW. Choose the fix by which side is the odd one out. When the pictures agree with each other and only the words disagree, the fix is FAULT[TEXT]. When the words agree across pages and one picture disagrees, the fix is FAULT[IMG] on that picture. Choose the single smallest change that makes the whole book make sense. Write one fault line per inconsistency, on the page where the listener first trips, never one line per page it touches; where the same words must also change on a later page, the sentence names that page. A FAULT[IMG] is the exception: write one line for each picture that must change, because each picture is redrawn on its own. A consistency fault that would make a listening child ask "but how?" or "where did she come from?" weighs at least MAJOR. This raised weight belongs to question 1 only: a fault found by any other question is weighed exactly by the weight scale below and is never raised.

Then, for every page, answer six more questions:
2. TOGETHER: taken together, do this page's words and picture tell one moment of the story a listener can follow? A picture may carry what the words do not say, and words may carry what the picture does not show.
3. CONTRADICTION: does the picture show something the words make impossible on this page — a character seated where the text has them standing, an object present that the text says is gone, an empty space where the words push through a crowd, or figures side by side facing the viewer where the words have them facing each other?
4. ONPAGE: does the event this page is for happen in the picture or in the words, or did it happen between pages?
5. EMOTION: where the words name a feeling, does any visible body language carry it?
6. CHANGE: could a five-year-old looking at the pictures alone say what changed since the previous page?
7. FUTURE: does the picture show a state a later page's text still establishes — a thing already freed, opened, reached or arrived? A small moment offset inside this page's own words is not a fault. A cover stands outside the story's order and may show any moment of it, its end included; never report one here.

Last, read the whole book three more times, each time as a different listener:
8. CHILD: you are a five-year-old hearing this book at bedtime. What would you want different? Look for the places where you would get bored, lose track of who is who or of what is happening, ask "why?" and get no answer, or feel let down by how it ends. These questions help you look:
{BUY_CRITERION}
9. PARENT: you are a parent reading this book aloud before you buy it. What would you want different? Look for the places where the main character does not solve the problem with their own act, where the price of failing is never said, where a sentence is hard to read aloud, or where the lesson is preached instead of shown.
10. SHOW-STOPPER: is there anything that would stop you giving this book to a five-year-old at all? For example, something frightening that is never comforted, unsafe behaviour shown as fine, a cruel or humiliating moment left unresolved, or a story that cannot be followed.
Each thing you would want different becomes an ordinary fault line on the page where its change belongs, with the change to that page's words or picture that fixes it. A gap in the whole book still belongs to one page: a price of failing or a reason that is never said goes on the page where the danger or the character first appears, with the sentence that says it, drawn from what the story already implies. {NO_NEW_ELEMENT} Never ask to shorten a passage that states nothing wrong, and never replace a regional word: the book's wording is its voice. Leave a thing out only when no change of words or picture on any page would settle it. A show-stopper weighs CRITICAL. Answers to questions 8 and 9 are weighed exactly by the weight scale below and are never raised. A FAULT[TEXT] from questions 8 or 9 weighs MINOR unless a listener cannot follow what happens without it; then MAJOR.

Answer these questions for every page, but do not write the answers down. Output only the faults they found.

Route every fault by its fix and weigh it. Write "FAULT[IMG][<WEIGHT>][<TYPE>]{ROUTE_TAG}: p<N> — <one sentence>" when a different image would fix it, and "FAULT[TEXT][<WEIGHT>]: p<N> — <one sentence>" when different prose would fix it; the sentence is the order to the editor, and {NO_NEW_ELEMENT} <WEIGHT> is one of: MINOR — a small detail is off and a listener would not notice; MAJOR — a described action, object or feeling is contradicted or wrongly shown, and a listener notices. Words describing what the picture simply does not show are not a fault; CRITICAL — the picture makes the words impossible, and a listener is confused; CATASTROPHIC — the page tells a different story: wrong characters, wrong place, or the opposite event. <TYPE> names what is wrong in the picture, exactly one value from this list: {FINDING_TYPES}. Use `wrong_action` when the picture shows a figure or creature doing a different kind of action than the text says (flying where the text says it hops); use `action_interaction` for a pose, a turn or a gaze.
