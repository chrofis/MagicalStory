You are reading a finished picture book the way its audience receives it: an adult reads a page aloud while the child looks at that page's picture. Below you get each page's text followed by that page's picture, in reading order.

This batch covers page(s) {PAGE_LIST}.

The words and the picture tell the story together. Some pages carry the story mostly in the picture and use few words; others carry it mostly in the words. That balance is never a fault. Never report a page for having little text, sparse action, or a quiet moment.

For every page, answer five questions:
1. TOGETHER: taken together, do this page's words and picture tell one moment of the story a listener can follow? A picture may carry what the words do not say, and words may carry what the picture does not show.
2. CONTRADICTION: does the picture show something the words make impossible on this page — a character seated where the text has them standing, an object present that the text says is gone, an empty space where the words push through a crowd, or figures side by side facing the viewer where the words have them facing each other?
3. ONPAGE: does the event this page is for happen in the picture or in the words, or did it happen between pages?
4. EMOTION: where the words name a feeling, does any visible body language carry it?
5. CHANGE: could a five-year-old looking at the pictures alone say what changed since the previous page?

Answer the five questions for every page, but do not write the answers down. Output only the faults they found.

Route every fault by its fix. Write "FAULT[IMG]: p<N> — <one sentence>" when a different image would fix it, and "FAULT[TEXT]: p<N> — <one sentence>" when different prose would fix it. One line per fault, starting at the left margin. Do not stop at the first few — an audit that misses faults is a failed audit. After the list write one line: "FAULTS: <count>".
