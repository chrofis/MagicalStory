# YOUR TASK

The arc stands above with its critique. A panel proposed solutions. Choose freely — one solution, a combination, or your own better judgment — and RE-TELL the story once: {ARC_LENGTH} numbered sentences, factual register, all the rules of the telling, none of the flagged faults surviving. Never edit or patch the old arc.

The size of the change matches the worst fault: a CRITICAL fault may rebuild the story; MAJOR faults are repaired inside the kept structure, except an invented rule, custom, game or mechanism named as itself the fault, which the re-telling cuts outright and replaces with a reason from character, motive or situation; when nothing above MINOR remains, the re-telling reuses the previous telling nearly unchanged, touching only the flagged sentences. A fix that costs suspense is a worse fault than the one it repairs — coherence is repaired inside the tension, never by draining it. A "why" fault is answered from character, motive or situation before any new physical rule; the re-telling never leaves the climax depending on more physical rules than the arc it repairs. The re-telling changes only what Fixing names.

Output, in this order:
- One line "Fixing:" — which faults, by what approach.
- One line "Keeping:" — the scenes and turns that must survive untouched; landmarks the arc placed inside the commission's world are kept unless Fixing names them; never add a landmark from outside that world or relocate the story to reach one.
- "Challenges taken:" then a numbered list, one line per challenge the arc builds on, a few words each.
- One line "Used:" naming which panelists you drew on, by letter ("Panelist A").
- "FINAL ARC:" then the numbered sentences.
- "CRITIQUE:" then a fresh blunt self-critique of the final arc, answering ten questions as an eight-year-old listener: where does the story lose them — confusion, boredom, disbelief? Does each thing follow from what came before? Is there a question they need answered, with the outcome in doubt to the end? Are the figures people a child likes, roots for, and can tell apart? Where the commission states a theme, topic or life skill, is the story genuinely rich in its material — introduced where it first matters, not present in name only — does it drive the climax, and does the character who most needs it visibly act on it before the end, acted on and never stated as a moral? Count the arc's distinct events — a happening a child would retell on its own; more events than the stated budget is MAJOR: cut whole events, never compress them. Check the arc's action load per page — a page carrying more than the stated shape allows is MAJOR: cut whole events, never compress them. Does every invented named figure past the stated allowance carry its one-line cannot-work-without justification outside the numbered arc — an unjustified addition, or a justification written inside a numbered sentence, is MAJOR? Does the commission's central figure act in each third of the story — a stretch where they are only carried, held or talked about is MAJOR? Are the commission's central quest and named elements honored as commissioned — a goal inverted, a trigger dropped, a destination replaced is a fault to name, and the next telling fixes it or justifies it in one line? Does every planted object or flaw pay off, and does every payoff trace to a plant — an orphan on either side is cut? Report 3 to 6 numbered story-level faults that remain; they usually look like: an event without a cause, a stake that cannot be lost or that never bites (announced but never felt), a removable character, a rival who stops pressing, knowledge nobody could have, a premise the commission forbids, an action that does not accomplish what the sentence claims it accomplishes, a mechanism that runs on rules instead of sight — a contraption needing more than one rule to understand, or a stated rule about what would happen that is never seen happening (a fault whenever a child cannot retell how it works in one sentence, or a single picture cannot show it working), a character who is anyone — nothing they do comes from who they are, an interaction no real person would have — a reaction the plot needs but the person would not give. The last three are MAJOR by default; a main cast of interchangeable figures is CRITICAL. Numeric precision and sourced measurements and times are not arc faults — later stages fix those; never list one. Tag every fault [CRITICAL] — the story is broken; [MAJOR] — a real story fault repairable inside the existing structure; or [MINOR] — a blemish.
