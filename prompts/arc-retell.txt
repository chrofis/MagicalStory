# YOUR TASK

The arc stands above with its critique. A panel proposed solutions. Choose freely — one solution, a combination, or your own better judgment — and RE-TELL the story once, from the beginning: {ARC_LENGTH} numbered sentences, factual register, all the rules of the telling, none of the flagged faults surviving. Re-tell the whole story; never edit or patch the old arc.

The size of the change matches the worst fault: a CRITICAL fault may rebuild the story; MAJOR faults are repaired inside the kept structure, and Keeping covers everything the faults do not implicate; when nothing above MINOR remains, the re-telling reuses the previous telling nearly unchanged, touching only the flagged sentences. A fix that costs suspense is a worse fault than the one it repairs — coherence is repaired inside the tension, never by draining it. The re-telling changes only what Fixing names; everything under Keeping appears unchanged.

Output, in this order — every declaration precedes the telling:
- One line "Fixing:" — which faults, by what approach.
- One line "Keeping:" — the scenes and turns that must survive untouched; landmarks the arc placed inside the commission's world are kept unless Fixing names them. Never add a landmark from outside that world, and never relocate the story to reach one.
- "Challenges taken:" then a numbered list, one line per challenge the arc builds on, a few words each.
- One line "Used:" naming which solutions you drew on, by panelist letter and solution number ("Panelist A Solution 2").
- "FINAL ARC:" then the numbered sentences, told under everything declared above.
- "CRITIQUE:" then a fresh blunt self-critique of the final arc, written by answering four questions as an eight-year-old listener: Where does the story lose them — a moment of confusion, boredom or disbelief they would notice? Is it logical — does each thing follow from what came before? Is it full of suspense — is there a question they need answered, and is the outcome in doubt to the end? Are the figures people a child likes, roots for, and can tell apart? Report 3 to 6 numbered story-level faults that remain, found by these questions. Faults usually look like: an event without a cause, a stake that cannot be lost or that never bites (announced but never felt), a removable character, a rival who stops pressing, knowledge nobody could have, a premise the commission forbids, a character who is anyone — nothing they do comes from who they are, an interaction no real person would have — a reaction the plot needs but the person would not give. The last two are MAJOR by default; a stated trait that reverses under trivial pressure, at no cost, is MAJOR — never MINOR; a main cast of interchangeable figures is CRITICAL. That nothing enforces an announced consequence is not a fault; that the consequence is never felt is. Numeric precision, the sourcing of measurements and times, and the physical plausibility of a mechanism that could be swapped without changing who does what and why are not arc faults — later stages fix those; never list one. Tag every fault [CRITICAL] — the story is broken: a premise the commission forbids, a stake that cannot be lost, an uncaused climax; [MAJOR] — a real story fault repairable inside the existing structure; or [MINOR] — a blemish.
