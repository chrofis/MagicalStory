# THREE TRIES (age 3)

The child this book is for is three. Write for that child. These rules override any instruction
elsewhere to build several challenges or a complicated plot.

[[plot]]**One problem, met three times.** The book has a single concrete problem, plain enough to point at.[[book]] It is a thing, a place or a creature that will not do what
is wanted, and it is different in every book. For a life-skill topic the problem is the outside event
that makes the skill necessary — something that happens in the world, never a feeling on its own, and
never the same request made three times.[[/book]] Three tries, no more. Each try is a different kind of attempt, not
more of the last one — pulling harder after pulling is the same try twice. The first two do not
work.[[/plot]]

**The child's own doing.** Before the third try the main character notices something about the
problem the first two missed. The third try comes from that noticing. Never luck, never a
grown-up stepping in to do it for them, never the problem simply going away.

**It resolves.** The third try works, and the problem is solved before the last page.

[[plot]]**Small noticing, not cleverness.** What the main character notices is the size of a three-year-old's
noticing. Never a plan, a trick, or a child who reasons like a grown-up.[[/plot]]

**Kindness is the motive.** Helping an animal friend or another child is the natural reason to keep
going. Heroism at this age is being kind and not giving up.

**A gentle obstacle, never a villain.** Nobody is unkind on purpose. What stands in the way is a
thing, the weather, or a size — not a person who means harm.

**Feelings named plainly.** Say what the main character feels in the simplest word for it: sad, then
helped, then happy.[[book]] One feeling per turn of the story, on the face and in what the body does.

**Food.** Only ordinary food a child of this age eats, named plainly. Never anything a small child
cannot chew or swallow safely.

**A life event is weather, not a task.** What changes around the main character, and what they still
do the same way. Never something they explain, put right, or are told the meaning of.

**Ending.** The problem is solved and somebody is glad. Warm and small — no prize, no ceremony, no
line about what it means or what the main character has learned.[[/book]]
