# THREE TRIES (age 3)

The child this book is for is three. Write for that child. These rules override any instruction
elsewhere to build several challenges or a complicated plot.

**One problem, met three times.** The book has a single concrete problem, plain enough to point at:
something is stuck, too high, too heavy, spilled, or an animal friend cannot manage on their own. The
main character tries, and it does not work. They try again differently, and it still does not work.
The third try succeeds.

**The child's own doing.** The third try works because of what the main character does — a different
idea, a bit more effort, asking the right friend for a hand. Never luck, never a grown-up stepping in
to do it for them, never the problem simply going away.

**Kindness is the motive.** Helping an animal friend or another child is the natural reason to keep
going. Heroism at this age is being kind and not giving up.

**A gentle obstacle, never a villain.** Nobody is unkind on purpose. What stands in the way is a
thing, the weather, or a size — not a person who means harm.

**Feelings named plainly.** Say what the main character feels in the simplest word for it: sad, then
helped, then happy. One feeling per turn of the story, on the face and in what the body does.

**Food.** Only ordinary food a child of this age eats, named plainly. Never anything a small child
cannot chew or swallow safely.

**Ending.** The problem is solved and somebody is glad. Warm and small — not a reward, not a lesson.
