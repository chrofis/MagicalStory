# PAGE PLAN

Before dividing, plan the pictures — simple pages first:
1. The story's emotional highlights: which moments earn a page of one character showing a strong feeling, close up?
2. Which pages show only a thing or a place — an artifact, a landscape, no people in frame?
3. Where is the one calm group picture — everyone present, nobody doing much — or a group seen from behind?
4. Where are three or more characters truly unavoidable? Bypass it where the story allows; keep it only where the story demands it.
5. Where does each close-up land, where does each ultra-wide?
Rough distribution: about two close-ups and two ultra-wides, the rest medium or wide — more close-ups when a moment earns one, but never just two shot types across the book.
Then one line per page: shot — who is in frame — the instant the picture shows — what is true after this page that was not before.
A complicated page — much action, many characters at once — is a fault: simplify the picture, never the story. A page after which nothing is true that was not before is filler: give it a change or give its material to a neighbouring page.

The plan line's instant is a single drawable moment:
- One moment — no montage, no before-and-after, no "then", no passage of time inside one picture.
- One action: one thing is being done, and everyone else watches it or does that same thing in the same place. Two characters given different actions at one instant lose one of them.
- At most three named characters in frame, and two carry a page better than three. The whole cast together is the portrait or arrival page, the book has at most one, and it holds up to {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE} named characters, wide or distant. Add no unnamed figures — no crew, crowd, onlookers or extra animals.
- One centre of action: never two vehicles, vessels or creatures each with its own state or its own crew.
- One camera position: an instant is seen from one spot — never inside a vehicle or structure and its exterior at once.
- Everyone stands on the ground. A figure up high only when the event IS that height — then one figure up there and no one else off the ground.
- Never two characters interlocked at one instant — a shared grip on one object, an object in flight, a hand-over: pick the moment before or after it. Never a thought or realization as the instant, or a climbing or stretching pose with hidden limbs; when the event is internal or mechanical, name its visible result instead (the box open, the found object held up).
- Never a pointing gesture as the instant: a look toward the thing, a turned head, a step toward it, or the thing held up instead.
- Never an exact count of like things in the instant: one, two, or several — a number above two belongs to the text.
- The action at the moment it happens, never the state that follows it.
- Name what is in the picture, never what is absent, empty or missing from it. When the absence is the story point, name its visible consequence, or make the empty place the page's subject.
- Never compose the frame — what fills it, how large something looks, where it sits; the shot type is enough.

Requirements:
- Each page follows causally from the one before it.
- A character in frame on a page is in frame on the next one unless a plan line says where they went. What a page establishes about a thing — stuck, heavy, hidden, out of reach — stays true until a later page changes it.
- Consecutive pages do not stage the same place with the same grouping, and one gathering is not split across two pages. A page keeps its characters at one depth together; separate a figure into the background only when the page's own instant requires the separation, and keep such pages few.
- Every page is visible: something happens that a picture can show. Any character who forces or blocks something is in frame where it happens.
- The main character appears on most pages; the others come and go in different combinations, not the whole group on every page. A close-up page is a waist-up moment (holding, reading, reacting), never a kneeling or floor action.
- When an AVAILABLE LANDMARKS list is present, an arc that names landmarks has chosen them — keep those, not others; an arc that names none has chosen none. A landmark may be scenery — a tower on the horizon, a bridge behind the action — and never relocates the story. No page names more than one place.
- A page shows a character about to act, decide or know what to do only when the next page shows that same character doing it.
- A physical challenge spends one page on the attempt itself — a character mid-effort against the obstacle. The decision before and the relief after never replace it.
- The emotional low point — a loss, a setback, the goal looking lost — gets a page of its own; the loss and its recovery never share a page.
- A page may put the subject alone in frame — the creature, the artifact, the place — when the moment is the subject's: hatching, breaking, glowing, arriving, with the children just outside the frame.
- A race or deadline the story runs on is scored mid-book at least twice — a page shows who is ahead or how much remains.
- The stake the arc has a character say aloud lands on an early page, spoken.
- A prop, pet or skill that solves a problem appears on an earlier page with its owner before it does the work.
- Stage a page with two people rather than three when two carry it.
- No plan line states what anyone learned, realised or now understands.
