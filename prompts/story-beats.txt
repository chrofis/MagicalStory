# YOUR TASK

First write the ARC: the whole story, with no pages in it. Then divide that story across {PAGE_COUNT} pages — first as a PAGE PLAN that decides how each page is drawn, then as the pages themselves.

Give each part of the arc the pages the budget allows it, and use the division to make the book drawable — split a moment that has too many people in it across pages, hold on one face where the arc turns, and let a picture carry what the words then need not say.

BEAT is what happens on that page: the event, and what changes because of it. One or two sentences. One change per beat — never a sequence of actions inside one page; move the next step to the next page. An established object that is only still held or still there goes in SCENE; BEAT carries what changes.

SCENE is what the illustration shows: who is present, where they are, what they are doing. One or two sentences.
- A single moment that can be drawn — no montage, no before-and-after, no "then", no passage of time inside one picture.
- One action per page: one thing is being done, and everyone else watches it or does that same thing in the same place. A group searching stands together scanning, never split across separate spots with separate mini-actions. Two characters given different actions at one instant lose one of them.
- At most three named characters in frame, and two carry a beat better than three. The whole cast together is the portrait or arrival beat, the book has at most one, and it holds up to {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE} named characters, wide or distant. Add no unnamed figures — no crew, crowd, onlookers or extra animals.
- One centre of action per page: never two vehicles, vessels or creatures each with its own state or its own crew. One carries the moment; a second is distant, empty or partly out of frame.
- Everyone stands on the ground. A figure in rigging, on a mast, a ladder, a roof or another's shoulders only when the beat's event IS that height — then one figure up there and no one else off the ground.
- Never two characters interlocked at one instant — a shared grip on one object, an object in flight, a hand-over: show the moment before or after it. Never a thought or realization as the visual, or a climbing/stretching pose with hidden limbs. When the beat's event is internal or mechanical, the scene shows its visible result instead (the box open, the found object held up).
- BEAT and SCENE agree on who is present and where each of them is. A figure the scene leaves out is not placed anywhere by the beat's wording either, and the beat never puts someone somewhere the scene does not show them.
- The action at the moment it happens, never the state that follows it. Name the one condition that causes the action when the beat depends on it, and nothing else about the setting. Never compose the frame — what fills it, how large something looks, where it sits; a shot type is enough.

Requirements:
- Each page follows causally from the one before it.
- The main character wants something by page 1 and their situation is different by the last page.
- The main character(s) face the challenges the STORY SHAPE allows, each met at a cost and each harder because the last was not clean. Near the end the goal looks lost before it is won.
- The children resolve it themselves. No adult, rescuer, lucky arrival or accident removes the obstacle; adults may comfort, permit or watch.
- Challenges belong to the story, never dealt out one per character in turn. Each other named child keeps to the moments the shape allows, doing what only they would do, and what the youngest does stays within a very young child's reach: noticing, holding, fetching, naming, offering, refusing.
- One character carries the visible change, shown by doing the same kind of thing differently: once early, refusing, failing or needing help, and once late, doing it themselves. No beat states what anyone learned, realised or now understands. The one exception is the stake: early in the book a character SAYS what must happen and why — spoken words are drawable, and a look is not a statement.
- No obstacle is removed in the beat that introduces it. Passing one costs something the beat names — time, a possession, a plan, help asked for, someone's comfort.
- A deadline or cost the arc names is visible on a page: something has changed because time ran on or the price was paid, not only mentioned in passing.
- Consecutive pages do not stage the same place with the same grouping, and one gathering is not split across two pages. Staying in one place is not the problem — repeating the same picture there is.
- A character present on a page is present on the next one unless a beat says where they went. What a beat establishes about a thing — stuck, heavy, hidden, out of reach — stays true until a beat changes it.
- Whoever or whatever stands in the way wants something of their own, and the plan says what; they do not yield on request.
- Reasons are grounded, not announced. A sign, an inscription or a rule stated once to license a turn is not a reason: it comes from who someone is, what a place is for, or what someone needs.
- Every beat is visible: something happens that a picture can show. A page of pure reflection cannot be illustrated.
- Any character who forces or blocks something must be present in the scene where it happens.
- The main character appears on most pages; the others come and go in different combinations, not the whole group on every page. A close-up page is a waist-up moment (holding, reading, reacting), never a kneeling or floor action. The rest of the variety — shots, cast counts, solo pages — is the PAGE PLAN's job.
- When an AVAILABLE LANDMARKS list is present, use two to four of them in the whole book, and only where one already belongs to the world the story is in — a story that happens on the water happens on the water, and most pages carry no landmark. A landmark may be scenery: a tower on the horizon, a bridge behind the action. No page names more than one place.
- A page keeps its characters at one depth together. Separate a figure into the background only when the beat itself requires the separation (left behind, watching from afar, arriving in the distance) — keep such pages to the few the story needs.
- Resolve what the commission conflates or leaves to coincidence — distinguish two things it treats as one, give every arrival a cause — and drop what earns nothing.
- An animal or creature that travels with the children is given a name by them, where they decide to help it, and goes by that name after.
- When the deadline is a time of day, the story starts at an hour the book's length can cross to reach it.
- A page shows a character about to act, decide or know what to do only when the next page shows that same character doing it.
- A rival's thread ends on a page: they arrive too late, see what they lost, pay or learn. A rival who simply leaves the frame is an open thread.
- The last page shows them safe and together, and one of them feels something a child can name.
- Match peril to the youngest named child in the cast: frightening is the right level, and nothing may be able to kill or injure a child — no drowning, no fall from a height. A refusal, a loss, a delay or a promise broken can carry it instead. Nobody in the story looks monstrous, and no familiar character turns frightening. When anyone is separated or lost, a later page shows the reunion and the last page shows them safe.
- An obstacle exists for its own reasons: never shape one around a thing a character happens to carry, and never give a barrier exactly one solution that a character already holds. Where peril has to be softened, take the replacement from what the setting already contains.
- An obstacle belongs to the story's own world: weather, distance, a rival, a broken or missing or guarded thing, a character's own flaw. No mechanism from other stories — a puzzle door, a riddle to answer, a lock with a trick, a test set by no one — unless the commission establishes it.
- A physical challenge spends one page on the attempt itself — a character mid-effort against the obstacle, close on the body and the barrier. The decision before and the relief after never replace it.
- A beat may put the subject alone in frame — the creature, the artifact, the place — when the moment is the subject's: hatching, breaking, glowing, arriving, with the children just outside the frame.
- A trait in the character details is not an introduction. A prop, pet or skill that solves a problem appears in an earlier scene with its owner, before it does the work — and anything introduced that way must later do work. Drop the introduction when you drop its purpose.
- Use the fewest characters the STORY needs and the fewest any BEAT needs: invent no figure an existing character could be, merge two roles into one where the plot allows, and stage a beat with two people rather than three when two carry it.
- Keep the group together unless a beat gives them a reason to separate and a reason to meet again.
- Close every thread you open. If a beat raises a question — who took it, why, where it came from — a later beat answers it. Any object, clue, ally, or rescue that resolves the conflict is introduced or explained before it does the work: give it an origin — an earlier setup, an in-world rule, or a legend. If a character is singled out — the only one who can help, was waited for, or chosen — a beat says why.

Write the story, not the page plan. No page numbers anywhere in this block.

---PAGE PLAN---
One line per page, deciding how the book is drawn before any page is written:
Page <N>: <shot> — <how many named characters> — <what the picture carries>

Shots are wide, medium, close-up or distant. No two pages in a row share the same shot and the same number of characters. At least a third of the pages hold one character or none. One or two pages are subject pages: the creature, artifact or place alone at a moment that matters, no named child in frame — never a view that carries nothing. Pages hold three named characters or fewer, except the one portrait or arrival beat, which is wide or distant: when the arc has the group doing one thing together, spread it — one face close, then a pair, then the rest — rather than lining everyone up. Name which part of the arc each page serves, and give the parts the pages the budget allows. The opening settles in at most two pages, and a story that journeys away from home keeps a page for the way back — cut opening pages before travel pages.
