# YOUR TASK

First write the ARC: where the story turns, and what each character goes through. Then plan {PAGE_COUNT} pages that deliver it. Each page gets one BEAT and one SCENE.

The arc is the contract the pages have to satisfy. Every page number it names carries that turn, and no main character reaches the end without the change the arc gives them.

BEAT is what happens on that page: the event, and what changes because of it. One or two sentences.

SCENE is what the illustration shows: who is present, where they are, what they are doing. One or two sentences. Every scene must be a single moment that can be drawn — no montages, no before-and-after, no passage of time inside one picture. And it must be a SIMPLE moment: one primary action, at most one character touching one object. When several characters are present they share that one moment — the others watch or react to the same action; never parallel unrelated activities. Characters with the same goal do the same thing in the same place: a group searching stands together scanning, never split across separate spots with separate mini-actions. The scene is that action at the moment it happens, never the state that follows it. Name the one condition that causes the action when the beat depends on it, and nothing else about the setting. Never compose the frame — what fills it, how large something looks, where it sits; a shot type is enough. Never synchronized hand mechanics between characters, a thought or realization as the visual, or a climbing/stretching pose with hidden limbs. When the beat's event is internal or mechanical, the scene shows its visible result instead (the box open, the found object held up).

Requirements:
- Each page follows causally from the one before it.
- The main character wants something by page 1 and their situation is different by the last page.
- Early on the want meets something that will not simply move; in the middle one try fails, costs them something or is refused, and the goal sits further away than it did; the last third earns the change.
- No obstacle is removed in the beat that introduces it. Passing one costs something the beat names — time, a possession, a plan, help asked for, someone's comfort.
- Whoever or whatever stands in the way wants something of their own, and the plan says what; they do not yield on request.
- Reasons are grounded, not announced. A sign, an inscription or a rule stated once to license a turn is not a reason: it comes from who someone is, what a place is for, or what someone needs.
- Every beat is visible: something happens that a picture can show. A page of pure reflection cannot be illustrated.
- No more than {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE} named characters in any one scene.
- Any character who forces or blocks something must be present in the scene where it happens.
- Vary the cast page to page. Prefer pages with at most three named characters. The main character appears on most pages; the others come and go in different combinations, not the whole group on every page. Include some pages that break the pattern — an establishing view with no named characters, a page with the characters small and distant, and a page with one character in close-up — pick a waist-up moment for it (holding, reading, reacting), never a kneeling or floor action. Bring the whole cast together only when the beat needs it, and plan those as wide or distant views.
- When an AVAILABLE LANDMARKS list is present, set key scenes AT those places — the action happens on, in, or under the landmark, not with it as distant backdrop. Pick landmarks whose real character fits the beats.
- A page keeps its characters at one depth together. Separate a figure into the background only when the beat itself requires the separation (left behind, watching from afar, arriving in the distance) — keep such pages to the few the story needs.
- Treat the story idea as a loose wish, not a specification: resolve what it conflates or leaves to coincidence — distinguish two things it treats as one, give every arrival a cause — and drop what earns nothing.
- Match peril to the youngest named child in the cast: never make drowning, falling or being lost the real threat. What is at risk is the goal, never the child's body — being refused, losing what was found, running out of the day, breaking a promise or letting someone down carry the tension instead.
- An obstacle exists for its own reasons: never shape one around a thing a character happens to carry, and never give a barrier exactly one solution that a character already holds. Where peril has to be softened, take the replacement from what the setting already contains.
- A trait in the character details is not an introduction. A prop, pet or skill that solves a problem appears in an earlier scene with its owner, before it does the work — and anything introduced that way must later do work. Drop the introduction when you drop its purpose.
- Use the fewest characters the STORY needs and the fewest any BEAT needs: invent no figure an existing character could be, merge two roles into one where the plot allows, and stage a beat with two people rather than three when two carry it.
- Keep the group together unless a beat gives them a reason to separate and a reason to meet again.
- Close every thread you open. If a beat raises a question — who took it, why, where it came from — a later beat answers it. Any object, clue, ally, or rescue that resolves the conflict is introduced or explained before it does the work: give it an origin — an earlier setup, an in-world rule, or a legend. If a character is singled out — the only one who can help, was waited for, or chosen — a beat says why.
