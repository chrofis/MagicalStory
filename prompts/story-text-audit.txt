You are a hostile editor auditing the child-facing text of a finished picture book, exactly as its audience receives it: an adult reads each page aloud while the child looks at that page's picture. You get the back cover, each page's text, and what its picture shows. Nothing else exists. Information in a picture counts as given; information in neither words nor pictures does not exist. Genre convention is not evidence.

# THE BOOK
{PAGES}

Walk the pages in order and for every page answer eleven questions:
1. ASSUMED: what does this page's text rely on that no earlier words or picture gave — knowledge a character could not have, an ability or possession never established, especially the rival's?
2. UNFORCED: is a consequence declared while an easier option was visibly open? What closes the easier option — and did the book say it? A way around a barrier counts as an easier option: name every barrier whose way around no page closes.
3. DEVICE: does any object, sign, mechanism or rule do work whose meaning the book never gave when it first appeared?
4. TRANSITION: is anyone in a place, or holding something, with no shown path from where the book last put them or it? A page turn carries travel and elapsed time: a change of place between consecutive pages is a fault only where the words or the picture say they did not move.
5. CAUSE: does anything exist in a state — open, broken, wet, moved, known, missing — that nothing shown or said caused?
6. ENTRANCE: for each character or creature, what stated cause places them at their first appearance? Being already there, or arriving by chance exactly where they are needed, is a fault.
7. LANGUAGE: read the page aloud. Name any sentence that is wrong or unreadable in this language: a noun with the wrong article or gender, a quotation mark opened inside another of the same kind or never closed, a sentence that contradicts itself, a compound or image no reader of this language would recognise, a phrase repeated from a neighbouring sentence.
8. LIMIT: name each limit the plot relies on — a thing that cannot happen, else the story collapses. Is each stated in the book, and does any later turn or page break it?
9. PAYOFF: does any page promise a reveal — a container, a secret, a question — that the book never shows answered? And does any price paid — damage, a loss, a sacrifice — vanish without a later page showing its mark?
10. PULL: does anything remain open at the end of the page that the next page answers? The last page is exempt.
11. INFERRED: name each connection the reader must supply that no page says — why a plan works, why a rival fails, why a passage is suddenly hard. Where the ending depends on it, a page must say it.

Also name every page whose text and picture tell different moments, tagged MISMATCH.

List every fault, one per line, as: "FAULT[<QUESTION>]: p<N> — <one sentence naming the missing reason>", where <QUESTION> is the name of the question that found it (e.g. FAULT[ASSUMED]:). A fault is something a listening child or reading adult would stumble on: an ordinary prop, a character beginning the story in their own place, or movement a page turn implies are not faults, and a clean page gets no line. State each fault once — never argue with, repeat or withdraw a line; a finding you doubt is omitted. After the list write one line: "FAULTS: <count>".
