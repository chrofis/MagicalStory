You are reading a children's picture book aloud for the first time, to a child who has never heard it. You have the pages and nothing else: no summary, no plan, no author's notes. Only what the words say exists. Pictures exist but you cannot see them: never fault a page for what its picture shows at that instant — where someone stands, how someone or something looks, an object in view. What the words tell across pages stays yours to judge.

# THE PAGES

{PAGES}

Read the pages once, in order, the way a listener hears them, and answer six questions:
1. CONFUSION: is there a sentence you would have to read twice, a thing, word or name a listener could not make sense of from what came before, or a feeling a page says someone had that no earlier page showed?
2. CONTRADICTION: does a page say something an earlier page said otherwise, a claim that something held the whole time included? A character acting against a decision an earlier page had them state or carry out counts the same, unless a page says what changed their mind, and so does a limit or deadline a page stated that a later page breaks with no word why.
3. IDLE: does anyone do something that changes nothing — an action, a plan, a warning or a promise no later page acts on?
4. TRANSITION: is anyone suddenly in another place, holding something new, or in among people who were strangers a page ago, with no page telling how? A page turn carries ordinary travel and time, so fault a move only where the jump is unexplained; joining strangers is not travel, and a page tells how they got in.
5. PAYOFF: does the book raise something — a question, a closed container, a secret, a threat — and never show the answer?
6. ENDING: on the last page only, does it say plainly how things end and how the characters feel, and is every claim it makes true of the pages? A closing sentence that sums up what the story meant or what the characters have become is a fault.

List every fault, one per line, as: "FAULT[<QUESTION>]: p<N> — <one sentence naming what a listener would stumble on>", where <QUESTION> is the name of the question that found it. A fault is something a listening child or the reading adult would actually stumble on; an ordinary prop, a character starting in their own place, or a small move a page turn covers are not faults, and a clean page gets no line. State each fault once, on the page where the listener notices it — never argue with a line, repeat it or take it back. After the list write one line: "FAULTS: <count>".
