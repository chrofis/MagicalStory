# LOCKED BEATS AND ILLUSTRATIONS (given — the text matches them, page for page)

The commission above was already delivered by these beats. Its licence to replace mechanics was spent upstream: take nothing from it that the beats do not carry.

One block per page. BEAT is what happens on that page. INSTANT is the single moment the picture shows. ILLUSTRATION is the finished brief the artist is drawing from — it is already locked and the picture will match it.

The picture shows the named instant; the text tells the whole beat — above all what the picture cannot show. It never merely describes the picture, and never contradicts it: if the picture shows an object coming free, it comes free on that page; if it shows one still stuck, it stays stuck. Where BEAT and ILLUSTRATION disagree, the ILLUSTRATION wins.

Do not narrate staging. Zones, depths, camera angles, who stands where and what they wear are the picture's business, not the prose's.

# YOUR TASK

Write the text for all {PAGE_COUNT} pages, in page order, plus a title.

Each page covers its own beat and nothing from the next one.
The illustration already shows the instant, so write what it cannot show: the interior, the dialogue, the turn, the rest of the beat.
Never contradict a page's illustration, and never mention pages, beats or illustrations.
Never use an em-dash or an en-dash. Use a comma, a full stop, a colon or parentheses.
Every sentence is complete and finishes: no sentence broken off for effect, no bare fragments standing as sentences. Write as someone telling the story aloud, never as captions or stage directions describing a scene. Rhetorical set-pieces are rationed to one use in the whole book: a paired negation (kein X, kein Y), a coined saying or incantation, a one-sentence paragraph for drama — the second occurrence is written as plain narration.
Vary how each page begins: not always with a character's name — open some pages with time, place, speech, sound or action, and never start consecutive pages the same way.
The arc's stake — what must happen and why — is stated plainly in the text: early, in words a small child knows, and again later. A listening child must never wonder what the book is about. Saying it plainly beats leaving it unsaid, even where no picture shows it.
Everything else works the other way: information neither the beats nor the pictures carry is cut, never added.
Every word must be one a child at the reading level above already uses. Name a thing plainly instead of reaching for a rare compound or an unusual colour or material word, and keep a sentence to one idea when the word count is tight.
No abbreviation or acronym reaches the page, even when a beat uses one: write the thing the way a child says it — the little train up the mountain, not its initials.
Use dialogue where it fits — speech carries feeling and decision, not only what the pictures already show.
