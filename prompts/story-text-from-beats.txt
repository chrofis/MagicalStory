# LOCKED BEATS AND ILLUSTRATIONS (given — the text matches them, page for page)

The commission above was already delivered by these beats. Its licence to replace mechanics was spent upstream: take nothing from it that the beats do not carry.

One block per page. BEAT is what happens on that page. SCENE is a one-line summary. ILLUSTRATION is the finished brief the artist is drawing from — it is already locked and the picture will match it.

Write only events the ILLUSTRATION supports: if it shows an object coming free, it comes free on that page; if it shows one still stuck, it stays stuck. If the brief has a character doing something, the text may name it or stay silent, but it must never contradict it — a reader sees both on the same page. Where they disagree, the ILLUSTRATION wins over the SCENE line, and the SCENE line over the BEAT.

Do not narrate staging. Zones, depths, camera angles, who stands where and what they wear are the picture's business, not the prose's.

{BEATS}

# YOUR TASK

Write the text for all {PAGE_COUNT} pages, in page order, plus a title.

Each page covers its own beat and nothing from the next one.
The illustration already shows the scene, so write what it cannot show: the interior, the dialogue, the turn.
Never contradict a page's SCENE line, and never mention pages, beats or illustrations.
Never use an em-dash or an en-dash. Use a comma, a full stop, a colon or parentheses.
Every sentence is complete and finishes: no sentence broken off for effect, no bare fragments standing as sentences. Write as someone telling the story aloud, never as captions or stage directions describing a scene.
Vary sentence openings between consecutive pages.
The arc's stake — what must happen and why — is stated plainly in the text: early, in words a small child knows, and again later. A listening child must never wonder what the book is about. Saying it plainly beats leaving it unsaid, even where no picture shows it.
Everything else works the other way: information neither the beats nor the pictures carry is cut, never added.
Every word must be one a child at the reading level above already uses. Name a thing plainly instead of reaching for a rare compound or an unusual colour or material word, and keep a sentence to one idea when the word count is tight.
No abbreviation or acronym reaches the page, even when a beat uses one: write the thing the way a child says it — the little train up the mountain, not its initials.
The title is in the story language, contains the main character's name, and does not spoil the ending.

Work in two steps.

Step 1 — write the analysis: one line per page naming what the reader learns and what changed for the main character, then name any page whose text risks repeating the page before it.

Step 2 — write the pages, fixing what the analysis found. A problem the analysis names must change the page it names.
