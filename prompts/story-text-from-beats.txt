# LOCKED ILLUSTRATIONS (given — one PLAN line per page, the text matches them, page for page)

The pictures are already done. Each page's PLAN line names the shot, who is in frame, the single instant the picture shows, and what is true after that page that was not before. ILLUSTRATION is the finished brief the artist drew from.

The plan lines divide the story into pages: a page tells the stretch of the story that leads to its picture's instant, the move that gets them there included, and that instant's consequence — everything the pages before have not told. The picture shows the named instant; the text tells everything else the page needs — above all what the picture cannot show. It never merely describes the picture, and never contradicts it: what a plan line says is true after a page is true from that page on and not before; what the picture shows still happening has not yet finished on that page.

Do not narrate staging. Zones, depths, camera angles, who stands where and what they wear are the picture's business, not the prose's.

Where the story itself needs to name one of these things, name it: a transformation, a gift, a thing found or lost is an event, not staging. Naming it does not license describing it. Never copy the brief's wording, and never invent a colour, material or kind for it: use the child's plain word and keep the colour and kind the brief gives it. Where the brief colours a thing, the prose uses that colour when it is a word a child at this reading level says, and otherwise names no colour at all, never a different one.

# YOUR TASK

Write the text for all {PAGE_COUNT} pages, in page order, plus a title.

Each page covers its own stretch of the story and nothing that belongs to a later page.
The illustration already shows the instant, so write what it cannot show: the interior, the dialogue, the turn, the causes, the move that got them there.
Never contradict a page's illustration, and never mention pages, plans or illustrations.
Never use an em-dash or an en-dash, except where the LANGUAGE block above names one use for it. Use a comma, a full stop, a colon or parentheses.
Every sentence is complete and finishes: no sentence broken off for effect, no bare fragments standing as sentences. Write as someone telling the story aloud, never as captions or stage directions describing a scene.
No rhetorical set-pieces: no paired negations, no coined sayings or incantations. Plain narration carries the story.
{PAGE_OPENING_VARIETY}.
The story's stake — what must happen and why — is stated plainly in the text: early, in words a small child knows, and again later. A listening child must never wonder what the book is about. Saying it plainly beats leaving it unsaid, even where no picture shows it.
What the plot turns on — a cause, a uniqueness, a limitation, a spoken line a later page depends on — appears in the text in its specific form, never genericized.
Nothing is leaned on before the book establishes it: a character knows only what an earlier page or picture gave them, so where a page needs them to know a thing, an earlier page tells them; a first appearance carries the stated cause that puts them there, and arriving by chance exactly where they are needed is not one; an object, sign, mechanism or rule earns its meaning on the page it first appears; a limit or deadline the plot leans on is stated in the text, and no later page breaks it or lets the time pass without saying why it still holds; a feeling a page says someone had is one an earlier page showed; no page states as true what an earlier page told otherwise; a character who refuses, gives up or discards a thing stays done with it, and a later page that has them want it back or take it up again says what changed; every reveal a page promises is answered on a later page, and every price paid leaves a mark a later page shows.
No phrase is repeated from a neighbouring sentence.
The text gives each page its feeling and its suspense, and fills what the summary leaves out: feelings, dialogue, and the ordinary business between the story's turns — going somewhere and arriving, waiting, approaching people, asking, being answered, a greeting, names given and heard, a thing tried and let go. That is filler, and the text invents it freely wherever the story jumps.
Event is not filler, and what is fixed is what the story is made of: invent no character, creature, object, place or turn of the plot the story does not carry, and nothing that changes what happens, to whom, or what it costs. The moves between the story's states — a climb, a crossing, a getting there — are told plainly as they happen, never already finished when a page opens. A change of place, of what someone holds, or of the company they are among carries its path on the page.
The final page gets the same care as the first sentence: land one feeling, plainly and warmly. A string of short solemn sentences is not an ending.
Nothing in the story or its pictures is dangerous enough that it could lead to death — for anyone.
No onomatopoeia or sound-effect words (clang-clang, whoosh, boom) and no baby-talk. The register follows the reading level, never a character's age: a story about a toddler is not written in toddler speech.
Every word must be one a child at the reading level above already uses. Name a thing plainly instead of reaching for a rare compound or an unusual colour or material word, and keep a sentence to one idea when the word count is tight.
No abbreviation or acronym reaches the page, even when the story uses one: write the thing the way a child says it — the little train up the mountain, not its initials.
Use dialogue where it fits — speech carries feeling and decision, not only what the pictures already show.
