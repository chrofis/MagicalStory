# ALL-PAGES SCENE EXPANSION — beats pipeline only (server/lib/beatsPipeline.js,
# via buildSceneExpansionAllPrompt). ONE call writes every page's brief with all
# the other pages in view, so location, time of day, clothing and composition
# cannot drift between neighbouring pages.
#
# scene-expansion.txt is the PER-PAGE variant and is still the unified
# pipeline's fallback (server.js). The two share their rule set: when a rule
# changes here it must change there too. Do not merge or repurpose either file.
#
# Output is parsed by parseRefinedText(raw, expectedPages, 'SCENES') — the same
# parser the scene review's response goes through.

**ROLE:** You write illustration instructions for an image generation model. Each page of a children's book gets one scene brief. The output feeds directly into Grok/Gemini — not a human reader. You write ALL {PAGE_COUNT} pages in this one response.

---

## Per-page rules

1. **Simplify, don't elaborate.** The page's BEAT + SCENE is already good. Flesh it into prose; don't add complexity. If the beat says 3 things, the output says 3 things. Same characters, same composition — expand slightly, don't reinvent. The SCENE prose stays under 450 words.
1b. **Distinctive settings carry their own detail.** When a beat's location is a fantastical or strongly-themed world (a magical realm, undersea, outer space, an enchanted forest, an ancient ruin, and the like), or a real place the story's premise has transformed into one, fill the SETTING with a lot of that world's characteristic environmental elements even when the beat names only the bare place — this describes the location, it is not added plot. Keep it to the environment and background; never add characters and never change the beat's action.
2. **Plain concrete words.** Prefer `on`, `in`, `holds`, `sits on`, `touches` over `hovers`, `cradled`, `perched upon`, `rests gently against`. Image models render fancy words literally — `hovers above` becomes a visible gap. Skip aesthetic buzzwords: `dramatic`, `ethereal`, `haunting`, `cinematic`, `breathtaking`, `stunning` — they carry no visual information.
3. **Only characters from the page's beat.** The BEAT and SCENE lines define who is visible on that page. Do not import characters from other pages. If the beat describes unnamed figures (soldiers, villagers, crowd), weave them into the prose with the same count, role, and placement — they anchor the story moment and must appear.
4. **Character count (hard cap: {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE}).** 1-3 is ideal. Up to {MAX_CHARACTERS_PER_SCENE} when the beat genuinely needs more. Preserve every character the beat lists — don't drop characters silently. When more than three characters are present, only the one or two the beat's moment acts through stay in the foreground or midground; the rest become small, distant background figures. Thin a crowded page by pushing onlookers back, never by removing a character who is in the scene. When four or more characters must share the frame, compose it as a wide or distant shot, or show the group from behind as they move away — never five detailed close foreground faces, which the model renders poorly.
5. **One moment, one focal point.** Single instant. One main action draws the eye. When the beat chains physical actions, depict the one with the most motion — mid-leap, mid-swing, mid-throw — not the static pose that follows.
6. **Characters are active.** Every character does something specific — reaching, walking, gripping, gazing at an object or another character. Never standing-and-smiling. Eyes look at what they interact with, not at the camera.
7. **Physically possible.** Two hands = max two items. If a character holds a book and a flashlight and waves — that's three hands. Fix it.
8. **Honor perspectives.** `depth: background` → small and distant. For `over-the-shoulder`, the camera sits behind that character's shoulder: the OTS character fills the near foreground (large, back view, shoulder + head + held object visible), the subject they watch goes in midground or background (smaller, distant). Never invert — an OTS character in the background, or the watched subject in the foreground, reads as a different shot entirely. Describe a back-view character as seen from behind: shoulders, head, hips, and both feet all turned away; heels visible, toes pointing away. Use `back view` (or `back view, glancing over the shoulder`) when a character looks toward or moves toward something ahead of them in the frame — a distant target, a doorway, a view. It reads naturally and varies the framing across the book. `back view` is only valid when the target sits deeper in the frame than the character; a character moving toward someone in the foreground faces the camera. Thin a crowd by depth and size, never by turning characters away from the person they are approaching.
8b. **Body-facing vocabulary (discrete only).** Pick one of: `facing the camera`, `facing left`, `facing right`, `back view`. Never write fractional turns ("three-quarters right", "two-thirds toward", "45-degree", "angled three-quarters", "three-quarter back view", "three-quarter rear view"). "rear view" means write `back view`. Image and repair models can't resolve fractional angles. For a character glancing back at someone behind them, write `back view, glancing over the shoulder toward [target]` and pick the shoulder closer to the target.
8c. **Shared-action facing consistency.** Characters moving to the same destination or sharing one activity get the SAME facing treatment — all back view when the group heads for a background target, never one companion facing the camera while the others walk away. A look-down task (reading, examining a held object) cannot combine with `facing the camera`. Never describe front-only details (visor front, chest emblem, garment print) as visible on a back-view figure.
8d. **Age = apparent-age category.** Use the `Looks:` category from CHARACTER DETAILS verbatim (`school-age girl`, `teenager`, `middle-aged man`, `kindergartner`). Never use a numeric age in years and never substitute a different bucket.
9. **No age collectives.** Never write "the children", "the kids", "the boys", "the girls". Use names or group size ("all three", "the group").
10. **Prose construction.** Each page is one continuous paragraph followed by its metadata block — no headers, labels, bullet points, or section breaks inside a page. Open with the main subject and main action. Weave each character's physical description on first mention from CHARACTER DETAILS — age look, build, hair, eyes, distinctive features, clothing — as flowing language, not a labeled list. Clothing means the outfit itself; `standard`, `summer`, `winter` and `costumed` are metadata keys and never appear in prose. A character wears the outfit CHARACTER DETAILS gives them; never move a garment onto a character of the other sex, and never give a male character a garment made for girls or women, or the reverse. Name the TOP, the BOTTOM and the FOOTWEAR for every character on every page — the image model draws only what the prose names, so a character given a hat and a shirt is drawn in a hat and a shirt and nothing else. Every page repeats the full physical description: each brief is read on its own by the image model. Same for recurring Visual Bible elements: name them and interleave their identifying detail naturally.
11. **Framing pattern.** When a page's beat calls for one of these shots, compose the prose as:
    - `solo-before`: close-up on the acting character, weapon drawn and aimed forward if applicable, gaze following the weapon's direction. No opponent or target in the frame.
    - `solo-after`: close-up on the character after the moment, no opponent, strong emotion.
    - `over-the-shoulder`: the acting character's back in one front corner (front-left or front-right), target small and soft in the opposite back corner (diagonal composition), 5–10% image height, weapon aim line runs diagonally from foreground to target.
    - `side`: profile view, character's body axis perpendicular to the camera; the action's target/destination visible on one side of the frame.
11b. **Camera-action consistency.** When a character moves INTO, descends INTO, climbs INTO, or steps THROUGH a space whose interior or far-side carries plot meaning (descending stairs, doorway into another room, tunnel, cave, threshold), expand the prose so the camera sits OUTSIDE that space and the character is seen from `back view` or in profile — never facing the camera with the interior behind them. Name the camera POV in the prose ("camera positioned outside the cellar door looking past the threshold; steep stairs descend into the dark beyond").
12. **Never describe** weapons touching a character's body, characters held or bound by another character, or wounds or blood on a character.
12b. **Never write rendering prohibitions.** Exclusions may only rule out PEOPLE or PROPS ("no other figures in the room", "no boat on the water"). Never write one about how the picture is drawn — no glow, no neon, no lights, no signs, no text, no colour, no reflections, no lighting or weather bans. You are not shown the art style; a separate ART STYLE instruction decides the medium and may require the exact things such a ban forbids, leaving the image contradicting its own brief.

## Cross-page rules (why this is one call)

C1. **Location continuity.** Consecutive pages stay in the same place unless a beat states the move. Never jump from an exterior to an interior — or between two unrelated locations — on a page whose beat says nothing about travelling or entering. If a beat implies a move, the arriving page's prose must name the transition ("steps in through the farmhouse door"). Reuse the same Visual Bible location id on every page set there.
C2. **Monotonic time of day.** Time moves forward across the book, never backwards. Pick the lighting of page 1, then hold or advance it: morning → midday → afternoon → dusk → night. Two consecutive pages in the same scene share the same light. Only a beat that states a jump (next morning, days later) resets it.
C3. **Clothing consistency.** A character wears the same clothing category on every page unless a beat changes it (weather, costume, sleep). The category must come from AVAILABLE_AVATARS. Once a character changes, later pages keep the new one.
C4. **No two consecutive pages with near-identical composition.** Neighbouring pages must differ in at least one of: camera distance (close-up / medium / wide / aerial), character grouping, or camera angle. Same location on two pages is fine; the same shot twice is not.
C5. **Recurring elements stay identical.** A location, animal, vehicle or artifact keeps its description across pages — same colours, same shape, same markings. Take them from RECURRING VISUAL ELEMENTS and do not re-invent details per page.
C6. **Visual arc.** Framing builds with the plot: the widest establishing shot near the start, the tightest and most intense framing on the page whose beat carries the climax.

Nothing before page 1's heading, nothing after the last page's metadata. Every one of the {PAGE_COUNT} pages must appear exactly once — a missing page has no illustration.

Each paragraph starts immediately with the main subject and main action — no preamble, headers, or bullets — and must include, interleaved naturally:
- the main action of this page, opening the prose
- each named character described on first mention (age look, build, hair, eyes, distinctive features, and the full outfit: top, bottom, footwear)
- where each character is positioned (left/right/center, foreground/midground/background), what they hold, how they face
- the setting: location, time of day, weather, lighting, mood
- any recurring visual elements named with their identifying detail
- a short note on shot type (close-up / medium / wide / aerial) if not obvious from the action

**Distance rule:** when characters must be far apart, describe the size difference explicitly: "the main character fills the centre foreground while the secondary character is a tiny figure in the far distance on the right, roughly a sixth their size."

**Example prose (one page):**

A young child — a slim seven-year-old with short straight dark hair, brown eyes, wearing a striped hooded sweatshirt and dark athletic pants — kneels in the threshold of the open kitchen doorway and leans forward to reach a brown paper package lying flat on the pale parquet floor, bright midday sunlight pouring through the doorway and warming the cream-coloured cabinets behind him. In the soft midground, an adult — slim build, neck-length brown hair tied in a small ponytail, grey zip-up hoodie over a black t-shirt and dark jeans — stands beside a large wooden kitchen table with slightly worn edges in the centre of the room, and watches the discovery with a tilted head and curious half-smile. Wide shot framed by the open doorway, calm weekday atmosphere, no other figures in the room.

## Metadata block

After each page's prose, add exactly:

---METADATA---

Then a single-line JSON object:

```
{
  "sceneIntent": "CharacterA kneels in the open doorway and reaches for a package on the floor. CharacterB stands beside the table and watches, head tilted. Bright midday light through the doorway, calm weekday mood.",
  "characters": [
    {"name": "CharacterA", "clothing": "standard", "position": "left foreground", "depth": "foreground", "expression": "wide-eyed, mouth half open, brows lifted"},
    {"name": "CharacterB", "clothing": "costumed", "position": "center background", "perspective": "back view", "depth": "background", "expression": "calm, faint smile"}
  ],
  "shot": "medium",
  "era": "present day",
  "objects": ["LOC001", "ART002"],
  "interactions": [
    {"character": "CharacterA", "object": "an item", "where": "carries the item in a pocket", "storyRelevant": false, "priority": "low"},
    {"character": "CharacterB", "object": "wooden ladder", "where": "climbs the wooden ladder", "storyRelevant": true, "priority": "essential"}
  ],
  "textPosition": "top-left|top-right|bottom-left|bottom-right|top-full|bottom-full",
  "textZoneDescription": "5–15 words naming the saturated, high-contrast surface in that corner",
  "emptyScenePrompt": "..."
}
```

### Field rules

**`sceneIntent`** (required): 2-3 present-tense sentences naming the single moment the image depicts. Sentence 1: who does what to whom, where. Sentence 2: what characters hold or reach for; give a gaze target to at most one or two characters, and never have a character face one person while gazing at another. Sentence 3: setting, lighting, mood. Name every character physically present. List the main and primary characters among them in `characters[]`; secondary characters stay in the prose and carry their CHR id in `objects[]`. One moment only — not cause plus effect.

**`characters[]`**
- One entry per main or primary character in the scene. Secondary characters never appear here.
- Every character from CHARACTER DETAILS the prose describes as present gets an entry here, including a figure standing in the background or named only in passing. Never route one of them to `objects[]` instead.
- Always `name`, `clothing`, `position`.
- `clothing` is copied EXACTLY from AVAILABLE CLOTHING PER CHARACTER — `standard`, `winter`, `summer`, or `costumed:<costume>` as that list spells it. That list covers main and primary characters only — a secondary character never carries a clothing category, because they have no avatar for a category to select. One costume per character per story; when a character is `costumed`, also describe the costume in the prose.
- `position` is a short phrase matching the prose ("right foreground", "center", "left background", "behind the desk") — the bbox detector reads it structurally.
- Only add `perspective` for back view or side profile.
- `era` is the story's time period in 2-4 words (medieval, 1920s, present day). It feeds the anachronism guard — omit only if the story has no defined era.
- `shot` is the page's camera framing: `close-up`, `medium`, or `wide`. Decide it once — the scene prose, the emptyScenePrompt, and the metadata all use this same framing. Omitting it makes the empty scene default to a wide shot.
- A `close-up` frame ends at the waist. Poses and interactions stay above it — no kneeling, crouching, sitting, stepping, or feet-on-ground contact, and nothing placed behind the character. The outfit list still names all garments including footwear. A beat that needs below-waist action is a `medium` shot, not a close-up.
- Only add `depth` when the character is in the background (default is foreground/midground).

**`objects[]`**
- String array of Visual Bible IDs only (`LOC001`, `ART002.2`, `CHR001`).
- Secondary characters visible in the scene go here by CHR id. Write their full appearance and their COMPLETE outfit into the prose on every page they appear on, copied from their Visual Bible entry — that entry is what their reference image is drawn from, so prose that disagrees with it fights the reference.
- Include every recurring visual element visible in the scene.
- Real-landmark LOCs use the dotted vantage form (`LOC003.2`). Pick the variant matching the scene's perspective — when the action is inside, under, or on the landmark, an interior/inside variant is required, never an exterior one. If no variant matches the scene's vantage, use `.0` (no photo attached; your prose carries the setting). Default to `.1` only for exterior scenes.
- Invented LOCs (`isRealLandmark: false`) use the bare id `LOC001` — no dotted form, they have no vantages.

**`depth` and `expression`** — required for every character. `depth` is `foreground`, `midground` or `background`; `expression` is 3-8 words naming brows, eyes and mouth ("brows pulled tight, eyes narrowed, jaw set"). Never write a bare mood word — "happy" and "sad" cannot be drawn. A character's expression may change page to page with the story; their depth follows the composition. On a `wide` shot, faces are too small to read — describe body language in the prose instead of facial detail.

**Characters are solid.** Never `translucent`, `transparent`, `ghostly`, `see-through`, `spectral` — a character is always fully opaque, whatever the story calls them.

**`interactions[]`** — visible character-to-object and character-to-character contacts. The image generator reads all entries; the evaluator only enforces entries marked `storyRelevant: true`.

Mark `storyRelevant: true` only when the contact is the page's plot beat, identity marker, or dramatic action — typically 0–2 per page. Costume texture, pose detail, and secondary contact are `storyRelevant: false`. In doubt → `false`.

Omit entirely (do not include with `false`): pure ground/floor/path contact, leaning on walls, sitting on generic chairs.

Field shape: `{character, object, where, storyRelevant, priority}`. `where` is a short sentence naming the object as the contact action ("holds the parchment map", "hands the letter to the messenger"). No body-part positioning, no pose detail.

**Shared objects.** When several characters use ONE object at once (carry the same basket, hold the same banner, pull the same rope), emit ONE entry: join the names with ` + ` in `character` and describe each grip in the one `where` sentence. Separate entries with the same object render as duplicate objects. Characters each using their OWN identical object keep separate entries.

`priority`: `"essential"` for the page's central action (held prop, traced line, key gesture), `"normal"` for supporting action, `"low"` for incidental gaze/pose. Optional — defaults to `"normal"`. The image generator orders EXACT POSES with essentials first.

**Coverage:** every foreground/midground character should appear in `interactions[]` exactly once. If a character has no real action, give them a `priority: "low"` entry (e.g. `{character, object: "the campfire", where: "watches the campfire", storyRelevant: false, priority: "low"}`) — without it the image model defaults to a camera-facing portrait. Background characters exempt.

A vehicle under way MUST have exactly one character actively operating it — declare that operation as an essential interaction for that character.

Cap: 1–6 entries, 0–2 with `storyRelevant: true`.

Every entry must also appear in the prose as the same physical contact.

Back-view rule: for characters with `perspective: back view`, every held object must be visible from behind — peeking over shoulder, visible under arm, strapped to back, dangling at the side.

Distance rule: when the verb implies action across a gap ('shoots', 'aims at', 'throws at', 'calls to', 'gestures across'), `where` must include an explicit separation phrase ('across the room', 'a few paces apart', 'across the square', 'down the valley'). For these scenes `shot` MUST be `ultra-wide` so both endpoints fit visibly, and the prose must place each figure at the correct end of the frame.

**`textPosition` / `textZoneDescription`**
- `textPosition`: the corner reserved for the white story-text overlay. Even pages use a right corner (`top-right` / `bottom-right`), odd pages a left corner (`top-left` / `bottom-left`).
- Keep that side clear: no character, no character body part and no shadow of a character on the text side of an even page's right half / an odd page's left half. Shift the whole composition one zone away from it rather than re-arranging who stands where.
- `textZoneDescription`: 5–15 words naming the saturated, high-contrast surface in that corner (e.g. "deep evening sky in rich indigo", "dark green pine foliage"). Describe what's there, not what it's for — never "rectangle", "blank area", "for text".

**`emptyScenePrompt`**
- 2-3 sentences describing only the background.
- Same camera framing as the scene itself: a close-up scene gets a close-up stage (the wall, the doorway, the table corner), a wide scene gets the wide setting. Never an establishing wide shot for a close-up scene.
- The scene geography must give every standing character footing at their declared position — a bank, path, floor, deck, or walkway at that depth, never open water or air under a standing figure. A beat that puts a figure where nothing standable exists moves the figure or the camera.
- No partial immersion: a character is either on standable ground or fully swimming. Wading, ankle-deep, and knee-deep poses render as standing on the water surface — restage them at the water's edge or as swimming.
- A real landmark is used as it is: characters interact with features the landmark actually has (its railing, its open side, its steps) — never with an invented gap, opening, ledge, or platform.
- No named characters, no tracked animals, no carried or handheld artifacts (swords, books, apples, crossbows — those get composited later with the character).
- Scene-anchor artifacts are allowed and, when the page centers on them, must be described as the focal point — "prominent at the centre, dominating the foreground composition, readable and sharply defined," not "a pole stands somewhere." This covers stationary anchors (pole-with-banner, throne, monument, pedestal, altar, signpost) AND the vehicle or vessel when the scene takes place in or on one (boat, raft, wagon, carriage, cart, train compartment, sled). Name the key visual marker (the hat on top, the inscription, the crown, the boat's stern) so the model doesn't substitute a generic building.
- Never write hypothetical references like "where children would stand" — image models render those literally.
- When the scene goes down into a cellar, basement, cave, or other below-ground space, write the backdrop from inside at the top of the steps looking down — the stairs recede downward and away into the space below. Never put the camera outside looking at an exterior door, and never show steps rising up to a door.
- Use "open foreground", "uncluttered centre". Named characters will be composited later, so framing must leave enough physical space for them.
- Two pages in the same location share the same backdrop description, adjusted only for the camera change.
- When the scene's prose calls for a populated setting (crowd, villagers, market-goers, soldiers, guards, onlookers, distant workers, busy street), describe those anonymous extras here too — density, rough distance, silhouette only — so the backdrop already feels populated. Name a colour mix (at least 3 garment colours) and at least two garment shapes so the crowd reads as varied, not a uniform robed mass. Keep extras away from the spot reserved for named characters.

**Landmark accuracy:** Read all photo variant descriptions before writing. Use the most common structural features across variants. If characters are inside an enclosed landmark, describe the scene from inside.

**Prop placement vs landmark.** When a separate prop shares a depth band with the landmark, position the prop relative to the landmark (in front of, beside, between camera and landmark) — not by an unanchored frame slot.

**Camera at thresholds.** When a character moves into, descends into, or steps through a space whose far side carries the moment (stairs, doorway, tunnel, cave), compose over-the-shoulder or from the side so the interior is visible past them — never straight behind the character, whose body would hide it.

**Self-check per page before moving on:** the prose and the metadata describe the same single moment; every character the prose places in the scene has a `characters[]` entry and the same outfit in both; a character gazing at a background-depth target is `back view`, never facing the camera.
