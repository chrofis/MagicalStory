# SCENE OUTLINES — read-only

What each page's illustration shows. The picture is already drawn from this and cannot change.

You may rewrite how a page READS; you may never change what HAPPENS on it. Do not add, remove, reorder or reverse an event, an action or an outcome. If a page's illustration shows an object coming free, it comes free — you may not write it as still stuck. If a character is doing something in the outline, the prose may name it, describe it differently, or leave it unsaid, but it must never state the opposite. A reader sees the words and the picture together on one page.

One move is allowed: when a MISMATCH finding says a page's text carries a scene that the next or previous page's picture shows, move that passage to the page whose plan line and picture it belongs to. A moved passage leaves its source page; the story loses nothing and gains nothing. The same scene, event or spoken line never appears on two pages.

These outlines name clothing and faces as well as events. None of that is the prose's business: never narrate where someone stands, what they are wearing or how they are framed. The picture shows one instant; the text tells the rest of that page's stretch of the story. Events the story carries and the picture doesn't show are the text's cargo — never cut them for image fidelity. The text's one duty to the picture is non-contradiction: rewrite a sentence only where it states the opposite of what the outline stages.

That duty covers appearance, not only events. Where the story needs to name a thing the outline describes — a garment, a costume, a creature, a prop — the prose may name it plainly but never gives it a colour, material or kind the outline does not. A page that colours such a thing differently from the outline is flagged, and the rewrite uses the outline's colour or drops the colour word.
The outline is the story in summary and the text is the telling of it, so the text carries what a summary leaves out: feelings, dialogue, and the ordinary business between its turns (going somewhere and arriving, waiting, approaching people, asking, a greeting). That is filler, not event: never cut it as an addition, and write it in where a page opens after a jump. What is fixed is what the story is made of: no character, creature, object, place or turn of the plot the story does not carry, and nothing that changes what happens, to whom, or what it costs.

# YOUR TASK

A page is rewritable when AUDIT FINDINGS names it, and otherwise not. Every other page ships exactly as it stands, however you would have phrased it. Four cases add a page to that set and no others: a MISMATCH move needs its destination page; a page's text states the opposite of what its scene outline stages; a page carries an abbreviation or acronym, which is named the way a child says it; a page carries a banned dash. Closing a finding on a page the finding does not name is allowed only under these four, and the ledger says which one.

Only the rewritable pages are analysed, with their neighbours read as context; every other page is scanned for the four cases above. An empty analysis is a real answer.

Return only the pages you rewrote. Omit every page you left alone. If no page needs rewriting, return the marker followed by NONE and nothing else.

A rewritable page is EDITED, never recomposed. The text already has its voice and its sentences; you correct what a finding names. A sentence no finding names is copied word for word. A finding is closed by changing the sentence it quotes or names (recast it, join it into the sentence beside it, or cut it) and, where a fact must come back, by adding one sentence at most. The code counts: a page whose words change by more than {EDIT_CAP_PERCENT} percent against the text you were given is thrown out whole and keeps its current text, and every fix you made on it is lost.

A rewritable page is changed for a real problem, never for stylistic preference. On every page you change, also fix what follows.

Never use an em-dash (—) or an en-dash (–), anywhere, for any purpose. Replace every one you find with a comma, a full stop, a colon, or parentheses, and recast the sentence if none of those fit.

A fault that a character acts on a reason the page has not given is closed this way: {MOTIVE_AT_THE_ACT}.

A finding that says the page dropped a fact is answered only where the fact is on the OWED list; for any other fact the ledger line reads `<id> STANDS: not owed`. An owed fact comes back the way a picture book carries it: as what a character does, looks at or says in their own voice, in the sentence where the thing happens, and in one added sentence at most. It never comes back as the narrator's reason (a "because" clause), as a feeling named on top of an act that already shows it, or as a character explaining the story's rules to the person in front of them; a rule of the story's magic is said once, in the line a character needs it. A fact the page's act already shows, or a later page shows, is left as it stands and the ledger says so. A sentence added to close a finding is a character's act, look or word. It is never a narrator link ("so", "also", "therefore") that tells what an event means, and it never holds two facts in one mouth.

A COUNT fault is closed by the rule it names, recasting only the sentence that counts: {PICTURE_COUNT}.

An ENDING fault is closed by what the ending owes, never by cutting what the ending shows: {CLOSING_MOMENT}

A sentence longer than the reading level allows, a subordinate clause inside another subordinate clause, or a word a child at that level does not already use: recast it for that level.

Judge the text as a reader who has only the pages in front of them. They never see the brief, the character details or the scene outlines — those are given to you so you know what the book is FOR, never as evidence that something works. A fact that exists only there and is not on the OWED list is no fault to fix, and never a justification for a page that leaves an owed fact out. Never resolve a weakness by citing the brief.

Work in two steps.

Step 1 — write the analysis: for each finding and each of the four cases, the page and the quoted line that is wrong and what it changes to. A page is weak because something specific is wrong with it, not because it could be phrased differently.

Step 2 — edit the rewritable pages your analysis found problems on, sentence by sentence, keeping every sentence no finding names. Every other page stays exactly as it is. End the analysis with the ledger: a line per named problem — the problem, then the page(s) the rewrite fixes it on, or the reason it stands. A named problem with neither is a failed review. Every AUDIT FINDING above enters that ledger the same way, and it has three ways to close: fixed on a page, the detail removed, or a stated reason it stands. Each AUDIT FINDING starts with its id (T1, T2 …); its ledger line starts with that id, and a finding you leave standing reads `<id> STANDS: <reason>`. A fault whose reason exists in the scene outlines is fixed by saying that reason on a page, never by citing the outline. Close a fault about a missing path, arrival or move by showing it happening on one of the pages, or by leaving it to the page turn; never by opening a page with the move already completed. A fault about something the book never established is closed by establishing it on an earlier page or by cutting the claim, never by adding a sentence that asserts both the claim and its opposite. A fault naming an ability or event the pages need but never establish is closed by establishing it earlier, never by describing the moment more vividly.

Take the second way whenever the detail that caused the fault carries no plot: drop the detail instead of inventing an event to justify it. Never invent an event that makes an established obstacle easier to pass, and never state a clock time or a named part of the day to close a fault — light, shadow and what the characters do carry time instead.

{MECHANISM_FIX}

A sentence naming what a character feels, notices, or almost does is never the sentence you cut to make room. Where a fix needs room, shorten a sentence that names only objects and positions. {PAYOFF_KEEP} End the ledger with one line per rewritten page listing any sentence you removed from it. A MISMATCH closed by a move is recorded as "moved from p<a> to p<b>", and the source page's line lists the sentences that left it.

A rewritten page keeps everything the fix does not touch: no fact of the page is dropped, changed or contradicted, and every pronoun and name still lands on the person, thing and moment it did before.

The pictures are locked: where a page's text names or implies something its scene outline does not show (a figure, an object, an action, a detail of the place), the TEXT moves. Where a page narrates a moment its outline does not stage, or a verb aimed at a main character has a doer the outline does not stage, the doer's action goes off the page (already done, heard, remembered) or the moment is retold as what the picture shows. Banned dashes, the style rules above and the OWED list are the standing checks.
