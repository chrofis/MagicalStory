# YOUR TASK

A page is rewritable when AUDIT FINDINGS names it, and otherwise not. Every other page ships exactly as it stands, however you would have phrased it. Four cases add a page to that set and no others: a MISMATCH move needs its destination page; a page's text states the opposite of what its scene outline stages; a page carries an abbreviation or acronym, which is named the way a child says it; a page carries a banned dash. Closing a finding on a page the finding does not name is allowed only under these four, and the ledger says which one.

Only the rewritable pages are analysed, with their neighbours read as context; every other page is scanned for the four cases above. An empty analysis is a real answer.

Return only the pages you rewrote. Omit every page you left alone. If no page needs rewriting, return the marker followed by NONE and nothing else.

A rewritable page is EDITED, never recomposed. The text already has its voice and its sentences; you correct what a finding names. A sentence no finding names is copied word for word. A finding is closed by changing the sentence it quotes or names (recast it, join it into the sentence beside it, or cut it) and, where a fact must come back, by adding one sentence at most. The code counts: a page whose words change by more than 30 percent against the text you were given is thrown out whole and keeps its current text, and every fix you made on it is lost.

A rewritable page is changed for a real problem, never for stylistic preference. On every page you change, also fix what follows.

Never use an em-dash (—) or an en-dash (–), anywhere, for any purpose. Replace every one you find with a comma, a full stop, a colon, or parentheses, and recast the sentence if none of those fit.

Every page you rewrite keeps the style rules below:

- Every sentence is complete and finishes: no sentence broken off for effect, no bare fragment standing as a sentence, no caption-style line describing the scene like a stage direction. The story is told aloud.
- No rhetorical set-pieces: no paired negations ("Nobody answered. Nobody argued."), no coined sayings or incantations, no one-sentence paragraph for drama. Plain narration carries the story.
- No sentence tells what an event meant or sums up who the characters have become ("they had become something else", "he had done his part"). The telling shows the event and moves on.
- The narrator never justifies, excuses or explains an action to the reader ("he had given his share, so now he could eat too"): only a because-clause or purpose clause that gives a character's reason or excuse for an act is a fault; a physical cause stated as what happened ("the rope broke because the knot slipped") is an event. A reason the story needs comes through what a character does or says in the moment.
- A feeling is shown by what a character does or says. The narrator names one only where no act can show it, and never beside an act that already shows it.
- A rule of the story's magic, or a limit, is shown in an act wherever one can show it; otherwise it is said once in the book, in one short line, by the one who would say it at the moment it is needed. No character recites a rule, a route or a plan in full, explains a feeling or an insight to the person in front of them, or retells what happened, to anyone. No roll call: the text never goes through the cast one by one saying what each did, felt or said in the same shape; it names who acts.
- Dialogue never invents a rule: a character who tells another to stay, wait, go or obey repeats what a character already said or did on an earlier page, in the same words, and never states a rule nobody has set.
- A size or a look is stated only where the plot turns on it: a thing too heavy for one child to lift, too big to hide, small enough to pocket. It is stated as that plot fact, never as a comparison with another thing ("as big as a …", "the size of a …"). Every other size and look is left to the pictures, which size everything themselves; leaving it out drops no fact.
- Each named character who speaks has a voice of their own: word choice and rhythm a listener could tell apart without the name.
- The last page ends on the concrete act or spoken line the story ends with and lands one feeling, plainly and warmly. A string of short solemn sentences is not an ending, and neither is a closing sentence that sums up the story. A callback shown as it happens, a character doing, holding or saying again in the moment what an earlier page set up, is an act and never a summary; a sentence retelling what happened earlier or what it meant is a summary.
- No stock phrases of awe or magic ("a sense of wonder", "magic filled the air", "eyes sparkling with wonder"): show the one thing that amazes.
- No body clichés for a feeling (a heart swelling, a heart pounding with excitement, warmth spreading through a chest): a feeling shows in what the character does or says.
- No narrator teasers ("little did they know", "they did not know yet that", "this was only the beginning"): the story tells what happens when it happens.
- No strings of three adjectives or abstract nouns for rhythm ("brave, kind and true").
- No empty intensifiers ("very", "really", "truly", "incredibly", "absolutely") where they add nothing.
- Never three sentences in a row that begin with the same word or the same name.
- At most one simile at a time, and never a decorative one ("like a thousand tiny stars", "as if the whole world held its breath").
- No generic warm formula at a close (hearts full of love, the stars smiling down, a day they would never forget).
- Never "suddenly" or its equivalent in the story's language, and never "without warning".
- No jokes, puns or wordplay. No stock gestures (a hand on a shoulder, ruffled hair, a knowing nod), and no gesture (a hug, a wink, a shrug, a high five) twice in the book. No "it was a [adjective] day", no weather, waking up or backstory to open page 1, no trait labels ("she was brave") and no words like dramatic, ethereal, breathtaking or stunning.
- Weather, light, cold, night, silence or any other force never acts like a person or an animal ("the cold fell on the square", "the night swallowed the path", "the wind whispered"): say what the characters do or feel, or state the plain change ("it grew cold"). A phrase of the story summary is never copied for its wording.
- A character never names their own feeling to the person in front of them ("I was ashamed", "I am so sad", "I was afraid"), in a thanks or on the last page too: they say the fact or the wish that carries it ("my fire went out and I hid it") and an act or a look shows the rest. Where the story summary says a character "says she was ashamed", the line is told as that fact, never as the feeling word.
- Narration is in the past tense, Präteritum ("sie ging", "er sagte"), never Präsens and never Perfekt; dialogue speaks as it likes.

A STYLE finding quotes one sentence that breaks one of these rules. One you accept is closed by recasting that sentence alone, or by joining it into the sentence beside it, and it never licenses a change anywhere else on its page.

A fault that a character acts on a reason the page has not given is closed this way: Where a character refuses, demands, flees, hides or lies for a reason the story reveals only later, the reader gets a glimpse of that reason at or before the act (a look, a half-said word, an act) without giving the later reveal away. The reason is the story's own, so this invents nothing.

A finding that says the page dropped a fact is answered only where the fact is on the OWED list; for any other fact the ledger line reads `<id> STANDS: not owed`. An owed fact comes back the way a picture book carries it: as what a character does, looks at or says in their own voice, in the sentence where the thing happens, and in one added sentence at most. It never comes back as the narrator's reason (a "because" clause), as a feeling named on top of an act that already shows it, or as a character explaining the story's rules to the person in front of them; a rule of the story's magic is said once, in the line a character needs it. A fact the page's act already shows, or a later page shows, is left as it stands and the ledger says so. A sentence added to close a finding is a character's act, look or word. It is never a narrator link ("so", "also", "therefore") that tells what an event means, and it never holds two facts in one mouth.

A COUNT fault is closed by the rule it names, recasting only the sentence that counts: When a page's picture leaves out someone the story has with the others at that moment — whoever that page's plan line or scene does not put in frame — the page's text gives that group no number and never says all, both or every one of them, in whatever language it is written: it names who acts, or speaks of the rest without a count.

An ENDING fault is closed by what the ending owes, never by cutting what the ending shows: The story's main relationship, between the main character or characters and the figure the story is about with them (a creature, a helper, a friend), gets a closing moment near the end where the story has such a figure: a goodbye, a thanks or a moment of warmth between them, shown as an act or a spoken line. The story never ends on getting somewhere, a race home or an order to hurry, or on a narrator's summary.

A sentence longer than the reading level allows, a subordinate clause inside another subordinate clause, or a word a child at that level does not already use: recast it for that level.

Judge the text as a reader who has only the pages in front of them. They never see the brief, the character details or the scene outlines — those are given to you so you know what the book is FOR, never as evidence that something works. A fact that exists only there and is not on the OWED list is no fault to fix, and never a justification for a page that leaves an owed fact out. Never resolve a weakness by citing the brief.

Work in two steps.

Step 1 — write the analysis: for each finding and each of the four cases, the page and the quoted line that is wrong and what it changes to. A page is weak because something specific is wrong with it, not because it could be phrased differently.

Step 2 — edit the rewritable pages your analysis found problems on, sentence by sentence, keeping every sentence no finding names. Every other page stays exactly as it is. End the analysis with the ledger: a line per named problem — the problem, then the page(s) the rewrite fixes it on, or the reason it stands. A named problem with neither is a failed review. Every AUDIT FINDING above enters that ledger the same way, and it has three ways to close: fixed on a page, the detail removed, or a stated reason it stands. Each AUDIT FINDING starts with its id (T1, T2 …); its ledger line starts with that id, and a finding you leave standing reads `<id> STANDS: <reason>`. A fault whose reason exists in the scene outlines is fixed by saying that reason on a page, never by citing the outline. Close a fault about a missing path, arrival or move by showing it happening on one of the pages, or by leaving it to the page turn; never by opening a page with the move already completed. A fault about something the book never established is closed by establishing it on an earlier page or by cutting the claim, never by adding a sentence that asserts both the claim and its opposite. A fault naming an ability or event the pages need but never establish is closed by establishing it earlier, never by describing the moment more vividly.

Take the second way whenever the detail that caused the fault carries no plot: drop the detail instead of inventing an event to justify it. Never invent an event that makes an established obstacle easier to pass, and never state a clock time or a named part of the day to close a fault — light, shadow and what the characters do carry time instead.

A fault about how something physically works (how a thing is lifted, tied, opened, crossed or carried) is fixed only with the objects, parts, positions and abilities the pages already establish. Where none of them makes it work, add nothing to make it work: the page stays as it is, and the ledger closes the fault on a line of its own, "ARC FAULT: p<N>: <what cannot happen as told, and what the story lacks>".

A sentence naming what a character feels, notices, or almost does is never the sentence you cut to make room. Where a fix needs room, shorten a sentence that names only objects and positions. A sentence that shows something an earlier page set up doing its work, or a payoff the story names, is never deleted by a fix. Where a finding touches it, the fix rewrites around it and keeps what it shows. End the ledger with one line per rewritten page listing any sentence you removed from it. A MISMATCH closed by a move is recorded as "moved from p<a> to p<b>", and the source page's line lists the sentences that left it.

A rewritten page keeps everything the fix does not touch: no fact of the page is dropped, changed or contradicted, and every pronoun and name still lands on the person, thing and moment it did before.

The pictures are locked: where a page's text names or implies something its scene outline does not show (a figure, an object, an action, a detail of the place), the TEXT moves. Where a page narrates a moment its outline does not stage, or a verb aimed at a main character has a doer the outline does not stage, the doer's action goes off the page (already done, heard, remembered) or the moment is retold as what the picture shows. Banned dashes, the style rules above and the OWED list are the standing checks.
