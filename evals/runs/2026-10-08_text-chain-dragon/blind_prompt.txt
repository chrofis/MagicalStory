You are reading a children's picture book aloud for the first time, to a child who has never heard it. You have the pages and nothing else: no summary, no plan, no author's notes. Only what the words say exists. Pictures exist but you cannot see them: never fault a page for what its picture shows at that instant — where someone stands, how someone or something looks, an object in view. What the words tell across pages stays yours to judge.

# THE PAGES

--- Page 1 ---
Das Laub flog hoch in die Luft. Levin, Julian, Max und Kiaan warfen es mit den Händen in die Höhe und fingen es wieder, rotes und gelbes, auf dem Lindenhof. Plötzlich zeigte Julian in den Himmel.

«Schaut!», rief er. «Grosser roter Vogel!»

Die rote Gestalt glitt langsam über die Türme des Grossmünsters. Max legte den Kopf in den Nacken. «Das ist kein Vogel», sagte er. «Der ist viel zu gross.»

--- Page 2 ---
Zwischen den Wurzeln der grossen Linde lag etwas Rundes. Levin kniete sich hin und schob das Laub zur Seite. Es war ein Ei, und es leuchtete orange.

«Es ist ganz warm», flüsterte er. Vorsichtig hob er es hoch. Von innen klopfte es zweimal.

--- Page 3 ---
«Kommt schnell!», rief Levin. Die anderen rannten herbei, und Kiaan beugte sich als Erster über das Ei.

Levin schlug sein Dinosaurierbuch auf. «Hier steht es», sagte er. «Dinosauriermütter sitzen auf ihren Eiern, damit die Eier warm bleiben.»

«Dann kommt die Mutter bestimmt bald», sagte Kiaan leise.

«Wir halten es warm», sagte Levin. «Wir warten, bis die Glocke vom Grossmünster den Abend läutet.»

--- Page 4 ---
Es wurde kühler auf dem Lindenhof. Julian zog seinen roten Schal vom Hals. «Da», sagte er. «Für das Ei.»

Die vier knieten sich ins Laub. Sie wickelten den Schal rund um das Ei, Lage für Lage. «Nicht zu fest», sagte Kiaan. «Aber es darf keine Kälte hinein», sagte Max.

--- Page 5 ---
Ein Schatten fiel über den Platz. Die rote Gestalt tauchte herab, das Laub wirbelte auf, und auf dem Hügel landete eine alte Drachin. Sie senkte ihren riesigen Kopf.

Max stellte sich breitbeinig hin. «Ich bin Max», sagte er laut. «Das sind Levin, Julian und Kiaan. Wie heisst du?»

Die Drachin knurrte tief. «Glutta.»

--- Page 6 ---
Julian wich einen Schritt zurück und stolperte. Er fiel gegen die Seite der Drachin und legte die Hand flach auf ihre Schuppen. Sie waren warm.

Julian zog die Hand weg. Er rannte hinter die Linde und drückte sich an den Stamm. Er sagte kein Wort.

--- Page 7 ---
«Das Ei gehört mir», sagte Glutta. «Ich habe es zwischen den Wurzeln versteckt, während ich eine warme Höhle suchte.»

«Wir haben nur auf die Mutter gewartet», sagte Levin. Er hielt ihr das Ei hin.

Glutta nahm es mit einer Kralle. Der rote Schal glitt zu Boden und blieb im Laub liegen. Levin hob die leeren Hände.

--- Page 8 ---
Glutta holte tief Luft und blies auf das Ei. Es kam nur grauer Rauch heraus. Ihre Kralle zitterte ein wenig.

Levin und Max sahen es. «Wir können dir helfen», sagte Max.

«Ich brauche keine Hilfe von Kleinen», knurrte Glutta.

--- Page 9 ---
Glutta machte zwei Schritte über den Weg. Ihre steifen Beine zitterten. «Meine alten Beine tragen mich nicht weit», brummte sie.

Sie breitete die Flügel aus, die auch steif waren. Vom See wehte ein kalter Wind. Er drückte sie zurück, und das Ei rutschte ihr aus der Kralle. Julian sah es hinter der Linde hervor.

--- Page 10 ---
Das Ei fiel auf den Weg und rollte los. Es rollte über den Kies und durch das Laub den Hang hinunter. Sein oranges Licht huschte über den Boden, genau auf die Wurzeln der grossen Linde zu. Max rannte hinterher.

--- Page 11 ---
Max warf sich nach vorn und fing das Ei mit beiden Händen. Es lag zwischen den Wurzeln, und es leuchtete heller als zuvor.

Kiaan war nachgekommen. «Schaut, hier liegen die Blätter ganz still», sagte er. «Und das Ei leuchtet heller.»

--- Page 12 ---
Glutta humpelte heran. Sie blies auf das Ei in Max' Armen, doch es kam nur Rauch. Das Ei wurde grau, und die Sonne stand tief.

Glutta setzte sich schwer ins Gras. «Mein Feuer ist weg», sagte sie. «Drachen wärmen Eier nur mit Feuer.»

Max sah auf das graue Ei. «Es ist ganz kalt», sagte er leise.

--- Page 13 ---
Levin trat vor die Drachin und hielt ihr sein Buch hin. «Schau, die Dinosauriermutter liegt um ihre Eier», sagte er. «Du könntest dich um das Ei legen.»

Glutta sah auf das Bild und wandte den Kopf ab. «Ich bin ein Drache und keine Henne», sagte sie.

--- Page 14 ---
Kiaan zeigte auf die Stelle zwischen den Wurzeln. «Hier bewegt sich nichts», sagte er. «Hier kann es bleiben.»

Levin häufte Laub zu einem Nest auf. Er holte den Schal und gab ihn Max. Max wickelte das Ei wieder ein und legte es ins Nest. Sie warteten, aber das Ei blieb grau.

--- Page 15 ---
Hinter der Linde kam Julian hervor. Er ging zum Nest, hob das Ei im Schal heraus und trug es mit beiden Armen über den Platz.

Er blieb bei Glutta stehen und drückte das Ei fest an ihre Seite.

«Warm», sagte Julian.

--- Page 16 ---
Das Ei begann zu glimmen, erst schwach, dann orange. Glutta sah es und riss die Augen auf. Sie nahm es vorsichtig in die Kralle und trug es zum Nest zurück.

Dort legte sie sich im Kreis darum und breitete einen Flügel darüber. Vom Grossmünster läutete die Glocke den Abend.

--- Page 17 ---
Die Kälte fiel auf den Lindenhof. Das Ei aber blieb orange. Es knackte, und die Schale sprang oben auf.

Ein kleiner Drache kletterte heraus. Er hickste, und ein warmer Schimmer stieg aus seinem Maul. Julian kniete neben ihm und lachte. «Funki!», rief er.

--- Page 18 ---
Glutta sah die Buben an. «Danke», sagte sie. «Mein Feuer ist ausgegangen, und ich habe mich geschämt.»

Die Buben beugten sich mit ihr über das Nest. Julian zog den Schal unter der Schale hervor und legte ihn um Funki. Funki kuschelte sich hinein.

Julian lachte. «Funki ist warm», sagte er.

Read the pages once, in order, the way a listener hears them, and answer eight questions:
1. CONFUSION: is there a sentence you would have to read twice, a thing, word or name a listener could not make sense of from what came before, or a feeling a page says someone had that no earlier page showed?
2. CONTRADICTION: does a page say something an earlier page said otherwise, a claim that something held the whole time included? A character acting against a decision an earlier page had them state or carry out counts the same, unless a page says what changed their mind, and so does a limit or deadline a page stated that a later page breaks with no word why.
3. IDLE: does anyone do something that changes nothing — an action, a plan, a warning or a promise no later page acts on?
4. TRANSITION: is anyone suddenly in another place, holding something new, or in among people who were strangers a page ago, with no page telling how? A page turn carries ordinary travel and time, so fault a move only where the jump is unexplained; joining strangers is not travel, and a page tells how they got in.
5. PAYOFF: does the book raise something — a question, a closed container, a secret, a threat — and never show the answer?
6. ENDING: does the last page say plainly how things end and how the characters feel, and is every claim it makes true of the pages? A closing sentence that sums up what the story meant or what the characters have become is a fault. A callback shown as it happens, a character doing, holding or saying again in the moment what an earlier page set up, is an act and never a summary; a sentence retelling what happened earlier or what it meant is a summary. Over the last pages: The story's main relationship, between the main character or characters and the figure the story is about with them (a creature, a helper, a friend), gets a closing moment near the end where the story has such a figure: a goodbye, a thanks or a moment of warmth between them, shown as an act or a spoken line. The story never ends on getting somewhere, a race home or an order to hurry, or on a narrator's summary. A book without that moment, or whose last page ends on getting somewhere, is a fault filed on the last page.
7. STYLE: check every sentence of every page against these rules the book was written to:
- Every sentence is complete and finishes: no sentence broken off for effect, no bare fragment standing as a sentence, no caption-style line describing the scene like a stage direction. The story is told aloud.
- No rhetorical set-pieces: no paired negations ("Nobody answered. Nobody argued."), no coined sayings or incantations, no one-sentence paragraph for drama. Plain narration carries the story.
- No sentence tells what an event meant or sums up who the characters have become ("they had become something else", "he had done his part"). The telling shows the event and moves on.
- The narrator never justifies, excuses or explains an action to the reader ("he had given his share, so now he could eat too"): only a because-clause or purpose clause that gives a character's reason or excuse for an act is a fault; a physical cause stated as what happened ("the rope broke because the knot slipped") is an event. A reason the story needs comes through what a character does or says in the moment.
- A feeling is shown by what a character does or says. The narrator names one only where no act can show it, and never beside an act that already shows it.
- A rule of the story's magic, or a limit, is shown in an act wherever one can show it; otherwise it is said once in the book, in one short line, by the one who would say it at the moment it is needed. No character recites a rule, a route or a plan in full, explains a feeling or an insight to the person in front of them, or retells what happened, to anyone. No roll call: the text never goes through the cast one by one saying what each did, felt or said in the same shape; it names who acts.
- Dialogue never invents a rule: a character who tells another to stay, wait, go or obey repeats what a character already said or did on an earlier page, in the same words, and never states a rule nobody has set.
- A size or a look is stated only where the plot turns on it: a thing too heavy for one child to lift, too big to hide, small enough to pocket. It is stated as that plot fact, never as a comparison with another thing ("as big as a …", "the size of a …"). Every other size and look is left to the pictures, which size everything themselves; leaving it out drops no fact.
- Each named character who speaks has a voice of their own: word choice and rhythm a listener could tell apart without the name.
- The last page ends on the concrete act or spoken line the story ends with and lands one feeling, plainly and warmly. A string of short solemn sentences is not an ending, and neither is a closing sentence that sums up the story. A callback shown as it happens, a character doing, holding or saying again in the moment what an earlier page set up, is an act and never a summary; a sentence retelling what happened earlier or what it meant is a summary.
- No stock phrases of awe or magic ("a sense of wonder", "magic filled the air", "eyes sparkling with wonder"): show the one thing that amazes.
- No body clichés for a feeling (a heart swelling, a heart pounding with excitement, warmth spreading through a chest): a feeling shows in what the character does or says.
- No narrator teasers ("little did they know", "they did not know yet that", "this was only the beginning"): the story tells what happens when it happens.
- No strings of three adjectives or abstract nouns for rhythm ("brave, kind and true").
- No empty intensifiers ("very", "really", "truly", "incredibly", "absolutely") where they add nothing.
- Never three sentences in a row that begin with the same word or the same name.
- At most one simile at a time, and never a decorative one ("like a thousand tiny stars", "as if the whole world held its breath").
- No generic warm formula at a close (hearts full of love, the stars smiling down, a day they would never forget).
- Never "suddenly" or its equivalent in the story's language, and never "without warning".
- No jokes, puns or wordplay. No stock gestures (a hand on a shoulder, ruffled hair, a knowing nod), and no gesture (a hug, a wink, a shrug, a high five) twice in the book. No "it was a [adjective] day", no weather, waking up or backstory to open page 1, no trait labels ("she was brave") and no words like dramatic, ethereal, breathtaking or stunning.
- Weather, light, cold, night, silence or any other force never acts like a person or an animal ("the cold fell on the square", "the night swallowed the path", "the wind whispered"): say what the characters do or feel, or state the plain change ("it grew cold"). A phrase of the story summary is never copied for its wording.
- A character never names their own feeling to the person in front of them ("I was ashamed", "I am so sad", "I was afraid"), in a thanks or on the last page too: they say the fact or the wish that carries it ("my fire went out and I hid it") and an act or a look shows the rest. Where the story summary says a character "says she was ashamed", the line is told as that fact, never as the feeling word.
- Narration is in the past tense, Präteritum ("sie ging", "er sagte"), never Präsens and never Perfekt; dialogue speaks as it likes.
   File one fault per sentence that breaks a rule, quoting the sentence whole: "FAULT[STYLE]: p<N> — «<the sentence>» <the rule it breaks, in a few words>". A sentence that breaks two rules is one fault; a sentence another question already filed is not filed again. One sentence that says who is present is not a roll call, and a thing said to be too big, too heavy or too small for what happens is a plot fact, not a size comparison.

8. LANGUAGE: read each page aloud and listen for the sentence that undoes itself. A thing is given two states in one breath (caught or held in a hand, then said to lie somewhere; someone leaving, then said to stay), a property is stated again for the same subject a sentence later with "also" or "too" or a repeated word, or a phrase is repeated from the sentence beside it. Quote the sentence whole: "FAULT[LANGUAGE]: p<N> — «<the sentence>» <what it says twice or against itself>".

List every fault, one per line, as: "FAULT[<QUESTION>]: p<N> — <one sentence naming what a listener would stumble on>", where <QUESTION> is the name of the question that found it. A fault is something a listening child or the reading adult would actually stumble on, or a sentence that breaks a style rule; an ordinary prop, a character starting in their own place, or a small move a page turn covers are not faults, and a clean page gets no line. State each fault once, on the page where the listener notices it — never argue with a line, repeat it or take it back. After the list write one line: "FAULTS: <count>".
