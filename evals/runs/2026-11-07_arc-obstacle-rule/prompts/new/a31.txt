# YOUR TASK

The critique above lists what the creator already sees; find what the creator MISSED, and report its three worst issues. The kinds of fault to look for — a checklist of what to look for, not a list to fill:
- LOGIC — an act against the want, motive, ability or limit the story logic gives its figure; an act that no motive line causes; a happening that causes nothing later; a fact that contradicts an earlier one; a "why don't they just …?" that a child would ask and the logic leaves open; a Facts limit the arc's own sentences break.
- ENTRANCE — the rule: Every figure is where a stated cause put them — never simply already there, never arriving exactly where they are needed. Figures who do not know each other meet on the page and learn each other's names before they act together.
- ASSUMED — the rule: Nothing is used that the arc has not given: a name is exchanged before it is used, a place shown before it is relied on, an ability or a possession established — with how they came by it — before it does work, and knowledge is held only by someone who could hold it. A figure who knows a place, a route or a fact, or who promises an act, a journey or a means, has a way of knowing it or doing it that the story shows, even where the premise implies it: a premise fact is never exempt. A plan, promise or appointment for after the ending is named only where the story set it up. Something relied on that the arc never gave breaks it.
- DEVICE — a rule, custom, game or mechanism that exists only to make a turn possible is a fault; so is a rule a child could not retell in one sentence, and one that does work whose meaning the arc never stated when it first appeared.
- CAUSE — something in a state (known, missing, blocked, exposed, open) that nothing earlier caused.
- CLAIM — an action that does not accomplish what the arc says, by the world's own physics and sight lines.
- REPLACEABLE — a turn that takes, breaks or blocks something the characters need, where the arc never says what stops them simply getting another, going round it, or doing without.
- SENSE — the rule: Every turn holds against what the story has already made true — how big things are, what they give off (sound, light, warmth, smell), how far apart places are, who is watching, what anyone present would plainly do. No turn leaves a reader asking "but why don't they just …?" or saying "that could not happen".
- PLACE — the rule: Every place the story stages is inside or outside, and a move between them goes through a way the place has: a door inside a building is reached from inside it, and a thing dropped, spilled or tipped lands where it happens — never on ground on the other side of a wall. An action that moves between inside and outside in a way the places forbid breaks it.
- FRAME — the rule: The arc decides its world frame and keeps it, and says which world or time the story is set in unless it is today's everyday world: (1) a make-believe world from the first page, where its wonders are ordinary and need no explaining; (2) the real world throughout, where nothing impossible happens; or (3) the real world first, then a crossing into the magical - the crossing is a staged moment with a cause the reader sees, an act or an event that turns the ordinary place magical, after which the magic is ordinary, and the story usually returns at the end, the way back a staged moment too. A story that opens at real places (a real town, real landmarks) opens in the real world, so it is frame 2 or 3 unless the commission itself puts it in a make-believe world. In the real world (frame 2, or frame 3 before the crossing) anything impossible that appears - a creature, a vehicle, a crew, a power - is a fault unless it arrives through the crossing. An arc that opens at real places with something impossible simply there, or that crosses into the magical with no cause the reader sees, breaks it. That issue quotes the sentence where the impossible thing appears.
- STAKES — the rules: The opposition never gives way just because it is asked, and no limit the story logic gives it takes away its power at the turning point. What keeps the main character from the want is the Opposition line of the story logic: the want and the Opposition are one problem, never two. The main character's own deciding act at the climax removes it. It breaks this rule when another figure undoes it with an act that was open to them all along, when it simply stops, or when chance removes it. Every question the story raises is answered by its end, including who keeps what was sought, won or fought over; and every deadline or loss the story sets is met or paid by then — the children are in time, or the cost the story named comes due.
- ENDING — the rule: The story's main relationship, between the main character or characters and the figure the story is about with them (a creature, a helper, a friend), gets a closing moment near the end where the story has such a figure: a goodbye, a thanks or a moment of warmth between them, shown as an act or a spoken line. The story never ends on getting somewhere, a race home or an order to hurry, or on a narrator's summary. An arc whose last sentences give the main relationship no such moment breaks it.
- CAST — the rule: Every child on the character list does at least one thing of their own that matters to the plot — never only present. Only where the book is too short for its cast do several children share one action, and no child is left with nothing. A commissioned adult has at least one act or line of their own too, and it is an act that changes what happens next: a permission, a warning or only watching does not count by itself. A want or quarrel a figure is given before the middle is answered by the ending, in an act or line of that figure, or it is not given. A figure on the character list who only stands by, an adult whose only part is a permission, a warning or watching, or a want or quarrel given before the middle that the ending never answers in that figure's own act or line, breaks it. That issue names the figure and quotes the sentence where they only watch, or the sentence that gives the want.
- PROMISE — where a TOPIC PROMISE is given, the arc breaks it. That issue quotes the promise and the arc words where it should have been kept.
- LANDMARK — the arc names a real place the REAL LANDMARKS list does not carry, or places a listed one at a vantage none of its photos shows and the commission's own words do not describe, or its LANDMARKS STAGED block lists a landmark that no arc sentence takes place at or beside. A place the arc invents for its own world is not one.
- ORPHAN — a plant that never pays off, or a payoff that traces to no plant.
