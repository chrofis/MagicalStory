# ARC 2 — "Die siebte Zeile"

**Setting:** Aldric's tower in the beech forest on the Uetliberg; the Zauberfest behind the gate of the Lindenhof, under the linden trees.

1. On a grey October morning Lukas practises the Herbstlichtspruch in front of Eli and gets stuck, because he knows only six of the seven lines, and he says out loud that without the seventh line he cannot finish his apprentice year; Aldric, loading the lantern cart in the yard, answers that the seventh line stands in the book behind the reading-room door, that the key has been missing since the summer storm, that an apprentice finds his own words, and that the door stays shut.
2. Frau Halder, who has led the lantern procession for forty years, takes the cart handle and tells the three that the gate of the Lindenhof shuts at the sixth bell and that the line does not wait for anybody, then walks off downhill behind Aldric.
3. Manuel says he will have the spell in twenty seconds and reads one off the Natel, but the words he finds turn the kitchen chair furry and cost an hour, and when Lukas tries his own six lines he chatters in the middle about the chair and the light dies before it starts.
4. Sophie carries the whole key drawer outside to try every key, trips on the step and tips the drawer into the leaves, therefore by three o'clock there are no keys left at all and she is so angry that she kicks the door.
5. Lukas lies down on the floor next to Eli to look under the door, notices that the hinge pins are on their side, and decides to take the door out although Aldric forbade it; Manuel holds the weight with both hands and puts the Natel on the windowsill to do it, Sophie slides the door sideways, and Lukas knocks the pins up with the nutcracker.
6. Lukas finds the book, reads the seventh line and says it back until it sticks, but the sun is already low, so they run — and the Natel stays lying on the windowsill, therefore they have no light on the steep wet steps through the forest.
7. Under the blinking red and white transmission tower Lukas ties Eli by his ribbon to Manuel's backpack so that the white ears stay visible in the dark; Manuel goes first, tests every slippery step and says where the roots are, and because there is no screen he actually listens while Lukas recites his seven lines twice.
8. They reach the Lindenhof after the sixth bell and the gate is shut; Frau Halder lets two late musicians through because they belong in the line, but not the children, and when Sophie shouts that this is unfair she folds her arms and turns her back.
9. Lukas hears the singing through the bars and cannot see Aldric anywhere in the crowd, therefore he sits down in the leaves with Eli and cries, certain that his year is gone — until the damp wind gutters out the lead lantern, the procession stalls, and Lukas stops talking, kneels over the heap of copper leaves outside the gate and speaks all seven lines without once breaking off, so that the leaves glow like embers and Frau Halder lights her wick at them and pulls the gate open.
10. Aldric has watched the glow through the bars and counts the year finished, Lukas tells him straight away about the door off its hinges and Aldric says they will set it back and fetch the Natel from the sill in the morning, and at the long table under the linden trees Frau Halder puts the lead lantern into Lukas's hands, Manuel sits without a phone and asks him how the spell actually goes, and Lukas, with Eli beside him on the bench, feels proud and very relieved.

**CRITIQUE:**

Premise figures:
- Aldric — wizard, teacher, absent
- Eli — stuffed elephant, companion

Invented figures:
- Frau Halder — lantern woman, gatekeeper
Allowed: 8. Written: 1.

Checks:
- Events: 6 against a budget of 5–7 (the charge and the closing time; the two failed shortcuts counted as one attempt-and-fail; the door lifted out; the dark descent; the shut gate; the dead lantern and the spell). OK
- Surplus facts: sentence 1 carries the deadline, the missing key, Aldric's rule and the ban on the door — four new things at once. MAJOR overload for one telling; the ban should arrive later, spoken by Sophie when Lukas reaches for the hinges.
- Invented figures: 1 written against 8 allowed. OK
- Central figure: Lukas names the problem and tries the six lines (first third), spots the hinges and pulls the pins (middle), ties Eli to the pack and lights the leaves (last third). OK
- Commission honored: tower, autumn forest, Zauberfest, first spell as the condition for finishing the year, Manuel and the Natel, Sophie losing things, the locked door with no known key, Eli, shortage of time — all present. The book is reached without a key, which the commission permits.

Questions:
1. The hinge trick is the best moment and the weakest link at once: a listener will immediately ask why nobody did that in the summer.
2. Mostly — the online spell costs the hour, the hour costs the daylight, the forgotten phone costs the light, the late arrival costs the gate. The dead lantern arriving right at the low point is the one link that is weather rather than consequence.
3. Yes: does he get through the gate in time? And it stays shut longer than is comfortable.
4. Yes — the chatterbox who must finally be quiet, the sister who tips out a whole drawer, the brother with his eyes down, and a stern old woman who is not wicked, only strict.
5. Very rich. The screen offers a shortcut and delivers a furry chair; the phone left on the sill costs them light, and the dark walk without it is the one conversation Manuel really has. Lukas's own flaw is an attention flaw, and the climax is won by not interrupting himself. The ending is people at a table and the phone lying in another building.
6. Eli does real work as the white marker in the dark and comes back on the bench. The nutcracker, the hinges, the forbidden door and the seventh line all pay off. Sophie's spilled keys shut down the easy option and then have no second use — thin but acceptable.

Faults:
1. [MAJOR] The hinge pins are the whole solution and lie in plain sight for months; the story must close that way on an earlier page — the pins are painted over and only give after Lukas soaks them, or the door was never anybody's business until today.
2. [MAJOR] The lead lantern going out is the hinge of the climax and nothing causes it but wind. It must be caused by something the reader has already seen: the damp leaves Sophie tipped over the cart, the rain that soaked the wicks on the descent.
3. [MAJOR] Frau Halder finally opens the gate herself, which puts the last turn in an adult's hand even though Lukas triggers it; the opening must cost her nothing she can withhold — better that Lukas's glow relights the line and she must follow her own procession through the gate he is standing in.
4. [MINOR] Three children going down a steep wet forest path in the dark is real risk and Manuel testing the steps is not quite enough; Aldric should name the steps before he leaves, so the caution has an origin.
5. [MINOR] Letting two musicians in and not the children is unfairness without a stated reason; one line from Frau Halder about who is in the line fixes it.
6. [MINOR] Sophie's spilled keys close an option but never come back; either the keys are found under the leaves at the end or her loss should cost something that returns.

Stronger: Arc 2 — the climax is caused by the hero's own change (the chatterer holds his tongue for seven lines) and the whole middle rests on a trick a child can retell in one sentence, while Arc 1 hangs its turn on a battery that dies at exactly the right second.

# YOUR TASK

The critique above lists what the creator already sees; find what the creator MISSED, and report its three worst issues. The kinds of fault to look for — a checklist of what to look for, not a list to fill:
- LOGIC — an act against the want, motive, ability or limit the story logic gives its figure; an act that no motive line causes; a happening that causes nothing later; a fact that contradicts an earlier one; a "why don't they just …?" that a child would ask and the logic leaves open; a Facts limit the arc's own sentences break.
- ENTRANCE — the rule: Every figure is where a stated cause put them — never simply already there, never arriving exactly where they are needed. Figures who do not know each other meet on the page and learn each other's names before they act together.
- ASSUMED — the rule: Nothing is used that the arc has not given: a name is exchanged before it is used, a place shown before it is relied on, an ability or a possession established — with how they came by it — before it does work, and knowledge is held only by someone who could hold it. A figure who knows a place, a route or a fact, or who promises an act, a journey or a means, has a way of knowing it or doing it that the story shows, even where the premise implies it: a premise fact is never exempt. A plan, promise or appointment for after the ending is named only where the story set it up. Something relied on that the arc never gave breaks it.
- DEVICE — a rule, custom, game or mechanism that exists only to make a turn possible is a fault; so is a rule a child could not retell in one sentence, and one that does work whose meaning the arc never stated when it first appeared.
- CAUSE — something in a state (known, missing, blocked, exposed, open) that nothing earlier caused.
- CLAIM — an action that does not accomplish what the arc says, by the world's own physics and sight lines.
- REPLACEABLE — a turn that takes, breaks or blocks something the characters need, where the arc never says what stops them simply getting another, going round it, or doing without.
- SENSE — the rule: Every turn holds against what the story has already made true — how big things are, what they give off (sound, light, warmth, smell), how far apart places are, who is watching, what anyone present would plainly do. No turn leaves a reader asking "but why don't they just …?" or saying "that could not happen".
- PLACE — the rule: Every place the story stages is inside or outside, and a move between them goes through a way the place has: a door inside a building is reached from inside it, and a thing dropped, spilled or tipped lands where it happens — never on ground on the other side of a wall. An action that moves between inside and outside in a way the places forbid breaks it.
- FRAME — the rule: The arc decides its world frame and keeps it, and says which world or time the story is set in unless it is today's everyday world: (1) a make-believe world from the first page, where its wonders are ordinary and need no explaining; (2) the real world throughout, where nothing impossible happens; or (3) the real world first, then a crossing into the magical - the crossing is a staged moment with a cause the reader sees, an act or an event that turns the ordinary place magical, after which the magic is ordinary, and the story usually returns at the end, the way back a staged moment too. A story that opens at real places (a real town, real landmarks) opens in the real world, so it is frame 2 or 3 unless the commission itself puts it in a make-believe world. In the real world (frame 2, or frame 3 before the crossing) anything impossible that appears - a creature, a vehicle, a crew, a power - is a fault unless it arrives through the crossing. An arc that opens at real places with something impossible simply there, or that crosses into the magical with no cause the reader sees, breaks it. That issue quotes the sentence where the impossible thing appears.
- STAKES — the rules: The opposition never gives way just because it is asked, and no limit the story logic gives it takes away its power at the turning point. Every question the story raises is answered by its end, including who keeps what was sought, won or fought over; and every deadline or loss the story sets is met or paid by then — the children are in time, or the cost the story named comes due.
- ENDING — the rule: The story's main relationship, between the main character or characters and the figure the story is about with them (a creature, a helper, a friend), gets a closing moment near the end where the story has such a figure: a goodbye, a thanks or a moment of warmth between them, shown as an act or a spoken line. The story never ends on getting somewhere, a race home or an order to hurry, or on a narrator's summary. An arc whose last sentences give the main relationship no such moment breaks it.
- CAST — the rule: Every child on the character list does at least one thing of their own that matters to the plot — never only present. Only where the book is too short for its cast do several children share one action, and no child is left with nothing. A commissioned adult has at least one act or line of their own too, and it is an act that changes what happens next: a permission, a warning or only watching does not count by itself. A want or quarrel a figure is given before the middle is answered by the ending, in an act or line of that figure, or it is not given. A figure on the character list who only stands by, an adult whose only part is a permission, a warning or watching, or a want or quarrel given before the middle that the ending never answers in that figure's own act or line, breaks it. That issue names the figure and quotes the sentence where they only watch, or the sentence that gives the want.
- PROMISE — where a TOPIC PROMISE is given, the arc breaks it. That issue quotes the promise and the arc words where it should have been kept.
- LANDMARK — the arc names a real place the REAL LANDMARKS list does not carry, or places a listed one at a vantage none of its photos shows and the commission's own words do not describe, or its LANDMARKS STAGED block lists a landmark that no arc sentence takes place at or beside. A place the arc invents for its own world is not one.
- ORPHAN — a plant that never pays off, or a payoff that traces to no plant.

An issue is a story problem, not a sentence: one problem that runs across several sentences is one issue.
