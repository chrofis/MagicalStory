# THE CREATOR'S ARC (its story logic, the arc, and the creator's own critique)

# ARC 1 — "La lanterna di Serena"

1. On Halloween night Serena, who is four, walks with Mamma through the autumn woods, past the foot of the tall wooden tower in the Sauvabelin forest, carrying the little pumpkin lantern she made herself, because Mamma has promised her the Villaggio delle Lettere, where every gate, lamppost and zucca wears a big letter and a little letter that shine all night.
2. They pass under the great pumpkin on the gate while the whole village is still glowing, but as they reach the square the letters go out one by one until everything is black, and Serena presses into Mamma's coat and says she is afraid of the dark.
3. Mamma sits down on the cold step because the dark holds her feet still, but she says out loud what must happen: they have to be outside the gate before the tower bell strikes midnight, or the gate stays shut until morning — and now not one lane looks like the way back.
4. Serena's lantern lights only one step at a time, so it cannot show her the lane or the path signs; but she sees a glint in the leaf pile, therefore she takes the broom leaning against the wall and sweeps.
5. Under the leaves lie a big A and a little a; she asks Mamma to lift her to the empty hollow in the tree — A come Albero — but Mamma cannot move, therefore Serena pushes the letters up with the broom handle herself.
6. The A blazes gold and pours light down the lane, so Mamma manages three steps, and Serena sings the first three letters of the alphabet song and feels brave.
7. A magpie drops out of the dark, snatches the A and the a straight out of the tree and carries them off; the lane goes black, the bell rings eleven, and Serena's bravery drains away.
8. Serena wants to cry, but she will not sit in the dark all night, therefore she takes Mamma's hand and leads her one lantern-step at a time towards the clinking sound down the alley.
9. In a crooked tree they find a nest heaped with all twenty-six letters, big ones and little ones, glowing faintly under the twigs, and the magpie stands on top of them and announces that her name is Rubina and that everything shiny is hers.
10. Serena asks politely for the letters, but Rubina refuses, because the heap is the only light she has and the night is far too black for a bird sitting alone.
11. Serena offers a chestnut, a copper leaf and the button off her coat; Rubina calls them dull, turns her back, and the echo of the bell dies away while nothing is gained.
12. Then Serena understands that Rubina is frightened of the dark exactly the way she is, therefore she holds out her own pumpkin lantern — her only light — and Rubina hops onto the handle and lets the letters go.
13. Serena feels small and scared without her lantern, but the loose letters glow faintly in Mamma's scarf, so she sits down and sorts the jumbled heap from A to Z by singing the alphabet song all the way through.
14. Up the lane she fits each letter into the place whose word it begins — C on the Cancello, F on the Fontana, L on the Lampione, P on the Ponte — and every letter she sets blazes and lights another stretch, therefore Mamma follows her step by step.
15. The M belongs under the porch, where a small spider has spun a web across the hollow, and Mamma backs away because she is frightened of spiders, therefore Serena lifts the web aside with a leaf, says good evening to the spider and presses the M home.
16. At the crossroads the path sign is blank, so Serena pushes in C, A, S, A, recognises the S from her own name, and reads her first whole word out loud, which makes the arrow light up towards the gate.
17. But the hollow in the great pumpkin above the gate is still empty, so the gate and the path beyond stay black and Mamma cannot take one step out, and Rubina calls down from the roof that the Z was too heavy and slipped into the leaves by the wall.
18. Serena rakes the black corner until the broom snaps in half, therefore she kneels and pushes her bare hands into the cold wet leaves — she has never been so frightened — but she sings the alphabet song to herself and keeps feeling until her fingers close on the big Z and the little z.
19. The pumpkin is far above her head and the broom is broken, therefore Serena holds the Z up and asks Rubina to fly; Rubina stares at the shiny thing for a long moment, but she has the lantern now, so she carries the Z into its hollow and the whole gate blazes.
20. Mamma walks out holding Serena's hand on the twelfth stroke of the bell, and on the forest path, with the coppery spire of the monastery showing above the dark trees, Serena walks home with no light at all, singing all twenty-six letters and telling Mamma that the dark is full of letters and she is not afraid of it any more.

Questions:
1. They wobble at the middle stretch of setting letters back, because after the trade nothing threatens Serena for several beats — the lane is just pleasant work.
2. Mostly yes: the lantern is traded, so the last search must happen in real dark; the broom sets a letter early, so its snapping late is felt. The one loose link is why Rubina never steals a second time once the letters are back on the posts.
3. Yes — will they get out before the bell? The Z going missing at the gate keeps it in doubt.
4. Serena is easy to like and easy to hear: she sings, she bosses her own fear. Rubina clacks and hoards. Mamma is warm but mostly sits; a listener would not confuse them.
5. The alphabet is the engine, not decoration: letters are the light, each one goes where its word begins, big and little forms are shown together, the alphabet song does sorting work and steadies Serena in the dark hole, and CASA is her first read word. It drives the climax.
6. Lantern, broom and song all pay off. The chestnut, leaf and button pay off only as refused offers; the spider is a plant with no later payoff.

Faults:
1. [MAJOR] The last physical act of the climax belongs to Rubina, who flies the Z into its hollow; Serena's choice causes it, but a child could retell the ending as "the bird fixed it".
2. [MAJOR] Rubina stops pressing the moment she has the lantern — through the whole lane sequence the rival applies no pressure, so the middle sags and her return feels summoned rather than earned.
3. [MINOR] The broom stands exactly where it is first needed, and the story never explains whose it is.
4. [MINOR] The spider beat is liftable: Mamma's fear opens and closes in the same breath and nothing afterwards depends on it.
5. [MINOR] Two rules are stacked — letters blaze only in their hollows, and Mamma can walk only where there is light. A child can retell each, but not both in one sentence.

Stronger: Arc 1 — the cost Serena pays (giving away her own lantern) is the very thing that makes the final dark real, so fear, choice and consequence run on one unbroken chain.

# YOUR TASK

The critique above lists what the creator already sees; find what the creator MISSED, and report its three worst issues. The kinds of fault to look for — a checklist of what to look for, not a list to fill:
- LOGIC — an act against the want, motive, ability or limit the story logic gives its figure; an act that no motive line causes; a happening that causes nothing later; a fact that contradicts an earlier one; a "why don't they just …?" that a child would ask and the logic leaves open; a Facts limit the arc's own sentences break.
- ENTRANCE — the rule: Every figure is where a stated cause put them — never simply already there, never arriving exactly where they are needed. Figures who do not know each other meet on the page and learn each other's names before they act together.
- ASSUMED — the rule: Nothing is used that the arc has not given: a name is exchanged before it is used, a place shown before it is relied on, an ability or a possession established — with how they came by it — before it does work, and knowledge is held only by someone who could hold it. A figure who knows a place, a route or a fact, or who promises an act, a journey or a means, has a way of knowing it or doing it that the story shows, even where the premise implies it: a premise fact is never exempt. A plan, promise or appointment for after the ending is named only where the story set it up. Something relied on that the arc never gave breaks it.
- DEVICE — a rule, custom, game or mechanism that exists only to make a turn possible is a fault; so is a rule a child could not retell in one sentence, and one that does work whose meaning the arc never stated when it first appeared.
- CAUSE — something in a state (known, missing, blocked, exposed, open) that nothing earlier caused.
- CLAIM — an action that does not accomplish what the arc says, by the world's own physics and sight lines.
- REPLACEABLE — a turn that takes, breaks or blocks something the characters need, where the arc never says what stops them simply getting another, going round it, or doing without.
- SENSE — the rule: Every turn holds against what the story has already made true — how big things are, what they give off (sound, light, warmth, smell), how far apart places are, who is watching, what anyone present would plainly do. No turn leaves a reader asking "but why don't they just …?" or saying "that could not happen".
- PLACE — the rule: Every place the story stages is inside or outside, and a move between them goes through a way the place has: a door inside a building is reached from inside it, and a thing dropped, spilled or tipped lands where it happens — never on ground on the other side of a wall. An action that moves between inside and outside in a way the places forbid breaks it.
- FRAME — the rule: The arc decides its world frame and keeps it, and says which world or time the story is set in unless it is today's everyday world: (1) a make-believe world from the first page, where its wonders are ordinary and need no explaining; (2) the real world throughout, where nothing impossible happens; or (3) the real world first, then a crossing into the magical - the crossing is a staged moment with a cause the reader sees, an act or an event that turns the ordinary place magical, after which the magic is ordinary, and the story usually returns at the end, the way back a staged moment too. A story that opens at real places (a real town, real landmarks) opens in the real world, so it is frame 2 or 3 unless the commission itself puts it in a make-believe world. In the real world (frame 2, or frame 3 before the crossing) anything impossible that appears - a creature, a vehicle, a crew, a power - is a fault unless it arrives through the crossing. An arc that opens at real places with something impossible simply there, or that crosses into the magical with no cause the reader sees, breaks it. That issue quotes the sentence where the impossible thing appears.
- STAKES — the rules: The opposition never gives way just because it is asked, and no limit the story logic gives it takes away its power at the turning point. The climax act of the main character answers what keeps them from the want, the Opposition line of the story logic. It breaks this rule only when that act answers a different problem and the Opposition is then undone by someone or something else: another figure through an act that was open to them all along, a chance event, or the Opposition simply stopping with no act of the main character behind it. An Opposition that yields, relents or is overcome because of the act of the main character does not break it. Every question the story raises is answered by its end, including who keeps what was sought, won or fought over; and every deadline or loss the story sets is met or paid by then — the children are in time, or the cost the story named comes due.
- ENDING — the rule: The story's main relationship, between the main character or characters and the figure the story is about with them (a creature, a helper, a friend), gets a closing moment near the end where the story has such a figure: a goodbye, a thanks or a moment of warmth between them, shown as an act or a spoken line. The story never ends on getting somewhere, a race home or an order to hurry, or on a narrator's summary. An arc whose last sentences give the main relationship no such moment breaks it.
- CAST — the rule: Every child on the character list does at least one thing of their own that matters to the plot — never only present. Only where the book is too short for its cast do several children share one action, and no child is left with nothing. A commissioned adult has at least one act or line of their own too, and it is an act that changes what happens next: a permission, a warning or only watching does not count by itself. A want or quarrel a figure is given before the middle is answered by the ending, in an act or line of that figure, or it is not given. A figure on the character list who only stands by, an adult whose only part is a permission, a warning or watching, or a want or quarrel given before the middle that the ending never answers in that figure's own act or line, breaks it. That issue names the figure and quotes the sentence where they only watch, or the sentence that gives the want.
- PROMISE — where a TOPIC PROMISE is given, the arc breaks it. That issue quotes the promise and the arc words where it should have been kept.
- LANDMARK — the arc names a real place the REAL LANDMARKS list does not carry, or places a listed one at a vantage none of its photos shows and the commission's own words do not describe, or its LANDMARKS STAGED block lists a landmark that no arc sentence takes place at or beside. A place the arc invents for its own world is not one.
- ORPHAN — a plant that never pays off, or a payoff that traces to no plant.
