# THE STORY (final — it was told, challenged and re-told; divide it, never retell or repair it)

1. On an autumn afternoon on the beach, Opa Hans gives five-year-old Emma his shell, which holds his greeting for the mermaid Maren and hums one low note, because Maren sings her answer into this shell once a year, and he tells her that all the leaves floating in the surf drift round the bay into Maren's garden.
2. So when Berta, the old turtle who has swum the same path every autumn for fifty years, crawls out of the surf between leaves and shells and lies down at Emma's feet, Emma understands that Berta wants her to follow, and Papa Daniel and Mama Sarah let her and seven-year-old Noah go until Papa lights the lantern on the jetty at dusk, when Opa will go home with empty hands.
3. Emma dips her feet in the surf and her legs become a fin, which the sea gives only to children and only on the day Berta comes ashore, so the grown-ups stay on the jetty while Emma pushes off from the sand and swims after Berta, Noah, who wants to be first at the wreck, jumps in after her and gets a fin too, and Mama calls to them to stay on Berta's path because the rocks in the surf are sharp.
4. Noah keeps to the path but swims ahead and touches the wreck first, a ship lying half buried in sand and hung with long kelp, while Berta, who knows only her path, swims slowly on past it along her path round the bay, and Emma, who wants to hurry to the garden behind the bay, cannot turn her and thanks her instead.
5. But as Emma swims past the wreck, the young octopus Fridolin, who hides everything that hums, looks out of a round porthole, says his name, asks to hold the shell, and when the children give their names and Emma clutches the shell tight, he snatches it, and when she begs for it back he only calls that nobody ever finds him and slips into the hold where he keeps his hidden things.
6. Noah, who hates to lose, lunges for him and grabs only water, Fridolin's ink spreads black through the water, the porthole is too small for Noah's shoulders and the planks around it are too hard to pull away, Berta is out of sight and the sky above is turning orange, so the shell looks lost and Opa will go home with nothing in his hands.
7. Because only Emma is small enough, Noah, who hates to lose the shell to an octopus, swallows his anger, ties strands of kelp from the wreck into a long line, knots one end around her waist, keeps the other in his hands and says two tugs mean he pulls her out, and Emma, who is afraid of the dark, goes in through the porthole anyway.
8. Inside the hold it is black, so Emma holds still until she hears the shell's faint hum, which the wood hides from Noah, and follows it along the line until her hand touches Fridolin, who is curled around the shell.
9. Fridolin says it is his now, but Emma, who has held on tight to everything all day, sees that he only wants to listen, so she offers to share: he may hold one side of the shell as they swim and hear Maren's song too, and Fridolin, who has no one to play with, comes out with her while Noah pulls the line.
10. Outside the water is black with ink and the sky is nearly dark, but Emma remembers what Opa said about the leaves, feels them brush her face all drifting one way, and leads Noah and Fridolin after them round the rocks until yellow and red leaves lie heaped on a coral garden lit by glowing anemones.
11. There the mermaid tells them her name is Maren, Emma gives hers and hands her the shell, and Maren listens to Opa's greeting, sings her answer into it, and gives the shell back to Emma, so the shell now holds her song.
12. When Fridolin hides behind a coral to see whether anyone will look for him, Maren swims straight to him and says he can hide in her garden every day, and Emma, who has held on to everything all day, hugs Maren, says goodbye and lets go.
13. At the edge of the garden Emma and Noah meet Berta's path again, where Berta glides by, and as Mama said they keep to it, past the sharp rocks to where it ends below the jetty, toward the gold glow of the lantern, which Papa lights just as Opa turns to go home, so Emma's head comes out of the water and she calls to him, and when they stand on the sand their legs are legs again.
14. Opa holds the shell to his ear, hears Maren's song, and hums the low note along while Emma sits on his lap in the lantern light and hums Maren's song with him.

Challenges taken:

2. Entering the water and the fin
3. Too dark in the hold and the black water
4. The path is hidden, so she follows the leaves
5. The lantern at dusk ends the time
6. Emma goes first into the wreck

A hint opens with the story sentence it changes, as (s7) or (s14-15): it applies where that sentence is told, never earlier, and what it changes there replaces what the sentence says.

ISSUE (s2): Dusk is narrated as the moment Opa goes home empty-handed, which contradicts him hearing Maren’s song in s14. → CHANGE: Treat empty hands as what happens if they miss the lantern, not as a stated outcome.
ISSUE (s3): Clingy Emma leaves the grown-ups without a backward glance, so her attachment never appears before she lets go of Maren. → CHANGE: Have her pause in the shallows until Opa waves her on, then push off.
ISSUE (s7): Noah’s temper vanishes in one swallow, which does not feel like a stubborn seven-year-old who hates to lose. → CHANGE: Let him mutter that the octopus will not win while he ties and holds the kelp line.

# MINI HERO'S JOURNEY (age 5)

The child this book is for is five. Write for that child.

**The full shape, in small.** Ordinary world, then a call that pulls the main character out of it,
then challenges that get harder, then a moment where all seems lost, then the turn, then home —
changed. Every one of those beats is on the page, in that order.

**A real low point is required.** Before the end there is a page where the plan has failed, the thing
is lost, or the friend is gone, and it looks like it will stay that way. Do not soften it into a
small setback and do not skip past it in a line.

**The hero's own idea turns it.** What saves the day comes out of the main character — something they
noticed earlier, a strength of theirs, a thing they were told and remembered. Never luck, never a
grown-up arriving to fix it, never a power handed over at the last moment.

**It resolves.** The turn comes before the last page and they come home changed. Never a story that
stops on the problem.

**A real antagonist is allowed.** Someone who wants something opposed to what the main character
wants, with a reason of their own. A twist, a sidekick who earns their place, and a setup on an early
page that pays off at the end all belong here.

**Competence.** The main character does things: decides, tries, fails, adapts. They are never carried
through their own story.

**The stake is the main character's own.** What is wanted and what is at risk belong to their own
life — never a piece of work set for them, an assignment, a submission, a competition entry, or a
place to be won in a selection. What stands in the way is something they can reach out and touch, or
someone who wants the opposite, never a procedure, a schedule or a system to be satisfied.

**Humour and mild peril.** A chase, a close call, a night out in the cold are fine. Nothing gory,
nothing cruel, nobody the reader likes is really harmed.

**Ending.** They come back to where they started and it reads differently, because they are different.

**The age owns the props and the subject.** What the main character wants, what stands in the way, and the objects the story turns on belong to the world of someone that age — what they handle themselves, where they go on their own, what counts as a loss to them. Never a want, a comfort or a plaything the reader has outgrown, and never stakes beyond what someone that age would be given.

# PAGE PLAN

THE BOOK'S TARGETS — every one is counted in code on the finished plan. A figure the STORY LOGIC names (new) is not commissioned and may share a page with commissioned characters or carry a page alone.
- At least 1 page of type face, and a face page shows a commissioned character.
- At least 1 page of type landscape or object; two are fine, ideally one of each.
- No two neighbouring pages share both their type and their characters.
- At most 2 pages of type group.
- The who column names commissioned characters and the figures of the STORY LOGIC, nobody else: a figure you invent is a fault.

Each page has a type, and the type fixes who is in frame:
- landscape: nobody. A place, the weather, a vessel or a distant sight.
- object: nobody, or one character's hand. One object.
- face: exactly one character, a close-up of their face.
- medium: one to three characters.
- group: four or more characters, all sharing one simple action.
The who column holds names only, or the word nobody: never a count, a group word or a description.

Before dividing, plan the pictures — simple pages first:
1. The story's emotional highlights: which moments earn a page of one character showing a strong feeling, close up?
2. Which moments earn a page with no people in frame? Only a subject the story has been driving toward — a discovery, an arrival, a payoff: the castle found after the long search. A place with no story weight never gets its own page. At least one page in the book earns this. Choose one whose drama is a place, the weather, a vessel or an object seen from afar; never a page whose drama is between people — a parting, a confrontation, tears, a promise, a handover. Those pages get faces, and so does every picture question 5 names as most wanted.
3. Which pages gather the whole cast, and what one simple action do they all share there? 
4. Where are three or more characters truly unavoidable? Bypass it where the story allows; keep it only where the story demands it. Where one is kept, the third character shares the page's one action — takes part in it, never does a second action and never only stands by.
5. For each act — setup, middle, ending — which picture does a child most want to see there, and which page stages it as its instant? The acts are the story's sentences in thirds: setup 1–5, middle 6–9, ending 10–14. The ending's own event — the reunion, the goodbye, the parting — is always one of them. A picture that falls between two pages, or that only a page's change reports as already done, is not staged.
6. Write the CAST block before the plan lines. One line per commissioned character: the page whose instant is their own action from the story, alone or with one companion, and 2 more pages they are in frame on. Then page 14, the last: the names of everyone the story keeps together at the end. Every plan line keeps what the block promises.
Then one line per page: type — who is in frame — the instant the picture shows — what is true after this page that was not before. The first field is the page's type: landscape, object, face, medium, group. Camera shots are chosen after the plan is final, never here.
A complicated page — much action, many characters at once — is a fault: simplify the picture, never the story. A page after which nothing is true that was not before is filler: give it a change or give its material to a neighbouring page. What is true after is a change in the story’s state, not the instant in other words: something now held, moved, opened, broken, learned, decided or agreed that was not true before this page. A fourth field that only redescribes the picture states no change.

The plan line's instant is a single drawable moment:
- One instant, one action: no montage, no before-and-after, no "then", no passage of time inside one picture; when a beat contains a change, pick the single moment that shows it. Never two actions by one character: plan the dominant one and leave the other out, never joined by "and then", "before turning to" or a trailing clause. One thing is being done, and everyone else watches it or does that same thing in the same place; two characters given different actions at one instant lose one of them, and so do two vehicles, vessels or creatures each with its own state or its own crew. The action at the moment it happens, never the state that follows it. A deed, its effect, and where the effect goes are three actions. Watching, standing and being present are not actions, and an effect that is the deed itself made visible at the same instant is part of the deed. When a plan line holds more than one action, split them across pages; a beat whose actions cannot share a picture gives one page the contact and the next page the second action, or the picture drops the secondary action and keeps the main event. The instant is at most 20 words.
- A page holds at most 6 named characters, a named animal that acts in the story counting as one; two or three carry it best. A page that gathers the whole cast is wide or distant, all of them sharing one simple action — boarding, hauling one line together — or seen from behind moving off. All of them watching one thing is a shared action only when they are seen from behind or over the shoulder, toward what they watch; facing the viewer it never is. Standing or gathering together while a feeling shows, or while the event happens behind them, is everyone present doing nothing — never a row of figures facing the viewer. One of them acting while the rest look on — a hand-over between two, one speaking, one laughing — is not a shared action: every one of them does the same thing. Add no unnamed figures — no crew, crowd, onlookers or extra animals.
- The second field is the complete cast of that picture. Every figure the instant stages — a person, or a creature the story tracks — including one watching from the background — belongs in that field, or is not written into the instant at all. A figure the picture shows only in part — a wing, a tail, a head or a hand reaching into the frame, a flank or a chest filling it — is in that picture exactly as if it were shown whole. Only a name without a body is context: the owner of a prop or a place, someone behind, beyond or outside what the picture shows. A figure named only in what is true after this page is not in the picture either: put them in that field or leave them off the page. A figure the previous page staged does not carry into the next one as background.
- One camera position: an instant is seen from one spot — never inside a vehicle or structure and its exterior at once.
- At most two height levels per picture: people, animals or objects at three or more different heights in one frame — one on the ground, one climbing the tree, one up in the tree; someone in the foreground and someone in the tree is fine. A whole cast carried together on one back or one boat is one level. Where a third level appears, stage the page on the two levels its event happens on and give the third level its own page.
- Several characters holding or moving one thing together is fine when that is the page's one action. A hand-over or an object in flight is never the instant: pick the instant before or after it. Never a thought or realization as the instant, or a climbing or stretching pose with hidden limbs; when the event is internal or mechanical, name its visible result instead (the box open, the found object held up).
- A figure trapped, cut off or unable to reach is given no means to end it: the obstacle's distance is named against the body ("the rim of the shaft three times their height above them", "the ledge a full arm's length beyond the fingertips"), never a grip, hold or footing that would let them out.
- Never a pointing gesture as the instant: a look toward the thing, a turned head, a step toward it, or the thing held up instead.
- Never an exact count of like things in the instant: one, two, or several — a number above two belongs to the text.
- Name what is in the picture, never what is absent, empty or missing from it. When the absence is the story point, name its visible consequence, or make the empty place the page's subject.
- Beyond the page's type, never compose the frame — what fills it, how large something looks, where it sits, or where the camera stands.
- A size or a look is stated only where the plot turns on it: a thing too heavy for one child to lift, too big to hide, small enough to pocket. It is stated as that plot fact, never as a comparison with another thing ("as big as a …", "the size of a …"). Every other size and look is left to the pictures, which size everything themselves; leaving it out drops no fact. A plan line names each thing plainly; the Art Director sizes it.
- The beat's main event wins the picture. A secondary figure or action is dropped before the main one is reduced to a shadow, a silhouette or a sound: the arriving creature is in frame as itself, and the reunion outranks the bystander.

Requirements:
- Each page follows causally from the one before it: no page stages a moment the story reaches only in a later sentence.
- What a page establishes about a thing — stuck, heavy, hidden, out of reach — stays true until a later page changes it.
- Consecutive pages do not stage the same place with the same grouping, and one gathering is not split across two pages. A page keeps its characters at one depth together; separate a figure into the background only when the page's own instant requires the separation, and keep such pages few.
- Every page is visible: something happens that a picture can show. A character whose action is what a page's instant works against — a way blocked, a place kept, a thing taken or withheld, a refusal — is named in that page's plan line.
- Emma is in frame on at least 7 pages; the others come and go in different combinations, not the whole group on every page. Every commissioned character gets a focal page of their own whose instant is the action the story gives them. The story's central figure, Maren, acts in each third of the book — it chooses, moves, speaks or changes something in a page's instant, never only carried.
- A character's focal page stages them alone or with one companion — two people sharing one action carry it — and stages them doing something visible. An internal decision gets a physical correlative — a held object, a step taken, a grip tightened; never a lone motionless figure.
- When an AVAILABLE LANDMARKS list is present, an arc that names landmarks has chosen them — keep those, not others; an arc that names none has chosen none. A landmark may be scenery — a tower on the horizon, a bridge behind the action — and never relocates the story. No page names more than one place.
- A page shows a character about to act, decide or know what to do only when the next page shows that same character doing it.
- A physical challenge spends one page on the attempt itself — a character mid-effort against the obstacle. The decision before and the relief after never replace it.
- The emotional low point — a loss, a setback, the goal looking lost — gets a page of its own; the loss and its recovery never share a page.
- A race or deadline the story runs on is scored mid-book at least twice — a page shows who is ahead or how much remains.
- The stake the arc has a character say aloud lands on an early page, shown as a visible act, never as the words said.
- A character's first page stages their arrival or a naming by someone present, shown as a visible act and never as the words said; a badge, a garment, a title or an epithet is not a naming. A character in frame before anyone says who they are is unplaced.
- A prop, pet or skill that solves a problem appears on an earlier page with its owner before it does the work, and nothing a page plants is left without a later page that pays it off.
- At most 1 page with figures in frame holds none of the commissioned characters; every other page with figures holds at least one.
- Page 1 opens on an action already under way, and each character's first page shows them doing something.
- When the story's ending has an event of its own, the last page stages that event as its instant.
- The last page shows the commissioned characters safe and together, unless the story itself separates them.
- No plan line states what anyone learned, realised or now understands.
